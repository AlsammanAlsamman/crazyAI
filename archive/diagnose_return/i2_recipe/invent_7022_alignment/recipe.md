CHOSEN SEED: A single ink-worm walks the eight desert winds and curls back across its own trail to test a junction twice.

1. Smooth a sand-tray flat. Lay the first knot-cord along the tray's north edge, one knot per symbol, running west to east; lay the second knot-cord along the tray's west edge, one knot per symbol, running north to south. Where each knot of one cord faces each knot of the other, scratch a furrow-point. This lattice of junctions is the whole board, and no junction may be added or moved once scratched.

2. At the tray's near corner — the junction before the first knot of either cord — drop a mound of height nothing. Along the whole north edge and the whole west edge, at every junction where one cord has not yet begun, drop a mound of height nothing also. These edge mounds are the worm's ground floor and are never kicked flat.

3. Agree on three fixed weights before the worm wakes, and write them on a stone set beside the tray: the grains gained when two facing knots are the same symbol; the grains lost when they are different symbols; and the grains lost for a sideways crossing, which is the price of letting one cord slip a step ahead of the other. The stone is never changed mid-crawl.

4. Wake the ink-worm at the near corner. From any junction it stands on, it may reach that junction only from three faces: from the junction behind it along the north cord's grain (a sideways crossing, one knot of the north cord let to slip), from the junction above it along the west cord's grain (a sideways crossing, one knot of the west cord let to slip), or from the junction diagonally behind it (both knots stepped together, the facing pair read and paid for by the stone).

5. At the junction it stands on, the worm reads the mound standing at each of those three faces and adds to each the grains the stone names for that manner of arrival: the diagonal mound plus the same-symbol gain or different-symbol loss according to the two knots facing at this junction; each sideways mound minus the slip price.

6. Take the tallest of those three tallies. If it is lower than nothing, take nothing instead — the worm may always begin its path fresh at this junction rather than drag a losing trail behind it. Drop a mound of that height at this junction.

7. If a mound already stands at this junction from an earlier pass, compare. If the worm's new mound is taller, kick the old one flat and sweep its sand away entirely — a beaten mound is kept nowhere and remembered by nothing. If the old mound is taller or equal, leave the old one and sweep the worm's away instead.

8. Beside the new standing mound, press one mark into the sand naming which of the three faces the worm came from to earn it, or no mark at all if the height was taken as nothing. Nothing else is written down anywhere; the sand is the only ledger.

9. Move the worm to the next junction — east along the row until the row ends, then down to the west end of the next row — and repeat steps 4 through 8. Every junction on the tray is visited this way.

10. Whenever the worm reaches a junction whose three faces do not all yet hold mounds, it doubles back, curling across ground already crossed, and works those faces first, then returns. No junction is ever settled before all three of its faces stand finished.

11. When the worm has settled the far corner and there is no junction anywhere on the tray holding a mound it could raise by returning to it, the crawl is done. Walk the tray once more and find the single tallest mound standing anywhere on it.

12. The height of that tallest mound is the number asked for: the agreement between the two strings. To see the reading itself, stand on that mound and follow its pressed mark back face by face, junction by junction, until you reach a junction with no mark — that unbroken trail of sand is the one continuous laying of the two cords against each other, slip and all.
