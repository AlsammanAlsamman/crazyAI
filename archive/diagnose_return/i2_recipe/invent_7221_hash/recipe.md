CHOSEN SEED: I fold one crease per mark, each crease angle set by both the mark and the crease before it.

1. Lay one huge sheet flat on the board, pink or orange, big enough that the spiral and snail-shell markings run corner to corner. Set the four wire birds along its left edge, beaks toward the paper. Have the pile of marks stacked beside your right hand, in the order it arrived, face down, so you can only ever take the top one.

2. Before any mark is read, make the starting crease: fold the sheet once along the line that runs from the first bird's beak to the farthest snail shell. This crease is the seed crease. Every sheet you ever begin gets this same seed crease, exactly here, so two people folding the same pile begin identically.

3. Take the top mark off the pile. Do not look at the one beneath it. Look at the mark and at the crease you made last — those two together, and nothing else, decide the next fold.

4. Read the angle this way: count the strokes in the mark, walk that many snail shells along the last crease from where the last crease ended, and fold from that shell toward the point where the last crease began. Press the fold flat. Where it stops is where the next one starts.

5. Carry the new fold's corner to each of the four wire birds in turn and touch it to each beak. If all four beaks meet the paper, the crease is set — go on. If any beak misses, unfold that one crease only, pull it a half-shell tighter, and touch the beaks again. Repeat until all four agree. Never unfold anything earlier than the crease you are working on.

6. Discard the mark you just folded. Throw it into the mud at the tide line. It must not be read a second time and must not be left where it could be compared against later.

7. Return to step 3 with the next top mark. Do this once for every mark in the pile, however many that is — one mark, one crease, no crease without a mark, no mark without a crease.

8. You know the marks are finished when your right hand closes on nothing. Do not add a crease to make the wad tidier and do not stop early because the sheet has grown stiff; the count of creases must equal the count of marks exactly.

9. Take the folded wad to the water's edge. Hold it under and press until it is thin, then thinner, then one hard dense corner no bigger than a coin. Hold it there until the breath you were holding runs out — that is the measure, and it is the same measure every time.

10. Lift out the corner. That is the token. It is finished when the corner takes no more pressing and holds its shape dry.

11. Everything else goes into the mud where the tide-boundary closes: every trimmed scrap, every half-shell refold that failed the beaks, every mark already thrown, the wet remainder of the sheet. Nothing but the coin leaves the water's edge.

12. To check a pile against a token later, fold that pile again from a fresh sheet by steps 1–10 and set the two corners side by side. Same corner, same pile. Any difference at all in the corners means the piles differed somewhere, and you need not know where.
