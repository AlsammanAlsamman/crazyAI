CHOSEN SEED: The bone-colored die-stone never resets between marks, so every fold carries the callus of all folds before it.

1. Clear the desk down to bare wood and sweep the numbered groove clean of old chalk. Set the wooden numbered keeps upright at the desk's far edge so the groove runs straight into them. Work only under the ship's cycle-light, which is one color at every hour — this matters, because a stone read under a changing light reads differently, and the token must not depend on when you stood there.

2. Lay the pile of marks along the groove in the order they were given to you, first mark nearest your hand, last mark nearest the keeps. Do not sort them, do not skip a blank, do not close a gap. The order is part of the pile; two piles of the same marks in a different order are two different piles and must fold to two different tokens.

3. Read each mark as a weight, not as a word. A mark's weight is its depth in chalk-dust measured against the groove's own numbering — never its meaning, never what it looks like it says. Write no weights down; you will take them one at a time and never need them again.

4. Take the bone-colored die-stone and seat it in the groove at the starting number cut into the wood at the near end. That number is the same for every pile anyone ever folds, and it is the only clean face the stone will ever show. Do not choose it yourself, do not vary it by mood or by pile.

5. Take the first mark. Press its weight into the stone's seated face — press, do not tap, and do not lift the stone off the groove. Lifting it clean is the one ruinous mistake: a stone set down fresh has forgotten, and a stone that forgets folds two different piles into one token.

6. Turn the stone a quarter through the groove while it stays seated, so it advances along the numbering by however far the press drove it. Its new seat is where it stops. Note nothing; the stone is holding it for you.

7. Take the next mark and press its weight into the face the stone is now showing — the callused face, not a clean one. The depth this press reaches is bent by how hard the last press bore down; a shallow seat takes this weight deep, a deep seat takes it shallow. This bending is the whole work. Then turn it another quarter, as in step 6.

8. Repeat step 7 for every remaining mark in the pile, in order, without pause and without ever lifting the stone. Do not rest partway and start again tomorrow from a fresh seating; if you must rest, leave the stone seated in the groove under the cycle-light, which will not have changed. A pile of five marks and a pile of a hundred thousand are folded exactly the same way — you simply turn more times.

9. Know you are finished when, and only when, the stone has turned once for every mark in the pile and there is no mark left between your hand and the keeps. Do not stop early because the seat looks handsome, and do not add an extra turn for luck — either one makes a token nobody else can fold again.

10. Lift the stone now, for the first and last time, and read its final seated number straight off against the wooden numbered keeps at the desk's edge. Read the number the keeps show, not the number you expected.

11. That reading, alone, is the token. Give it out.

12. Sweep everything else off the desk and throw it away: the groove-dust, the chalk residue, every intermediate seat you passed through, and the weights themselves. Nothing but the last number leaves the desk. If two folders follow these twelve steps on the same pile, they hand over the same token; if their piles differ by so much as one hair's width of chalk-dust on one mark, the bending in step 7 multiplies that difference at every turn after it, and the two tokens will bear no resemblance to each other at all.
