CHOSEN SEED: A rolling sphere serves as the sole carried memory across the whole ordered pile.

1. Lay the pile of marks out along the coiled limestone trail in the order they were given, one mark to one flagstone, from the trail's high mouth downward; do not sort them, do not skip a stone, and count them as you lay them so you know how many stones the sphere must pass.
2. Before the first mark, set a fresh sphere at the high mouth and press into its face the priest's opening shape — the same shape every time, for every pile, so that two makers working apart still begin identically. This sphere is now your only memory; you will keep no other note of anything.
3. Take the first mark's flagstone. Press that mark's shape into the sphere's face alongside the shape already there, so the two are held on the one face together — the carried weight and the new mark, combined, not set side by side in a row.
4. Release the sphere and let it tumble exactly the fixed count of turns down the mason trail — the same count for every mark, never fewer because a mark looks simple, never more because one looks stubborn. Count the turns aloud so you cannot cheat.
5. At each turn the sphere strikes a stalk and sobs once, cracking a hairline into itself. Read the angle of the crack it carries when the fixed count is spent, and take that angle as the sphere's new face. The face it had before is now dead: let it shrink away, and never look at it again.
6. Sweep the footprint left at that stalk, the stone dust, and any eggshell the trail shed off into the jungle's open plumbing. Keep nothing but the sphere and its one new face.
7. Walk the sphere back up to the high mouth. Return to step 3 with the next flagstone's mark, and do this over and over — press in, tumble the fixed count, read the crack, discard the old face, sweep the leavings — once for every stone you laid, in strict order down the trail.
8. Do not stop early when a face looks pleasing, and do not stop late: you are finished tumbling when the flagstone you just used is the last one you laid, and the count of tumbles you have made equals the count of marks times the fixed count of turns. That equality is how you know you are done and not merely tired.
9. After the last mark, give the sphere one final tumble of the fixed count with the number of marks itself pressed into its face — so a short pile and a long pile that happen to run alike still part ways at the end.
10. Take the hairline crack in the final stalk — that last, smallest one only — and lift it out as the token. Hand that over.
11. Throw the sphere itself into the plumbing with everything else. Keep no copy of any face it wore along the way; if someone wants the token again they must lay the pile out and roll it again from the opening shape.
12. To check your work: change one mark anywhere in the pile, even the first, even the last, and run the whole trail again. Because each tumble carried the whole face forward and not a sliver of it, the new token should share no recognizable line with the old. If the two tokens still look like cousins, your fixed count of turns is too small — raise it and roll every pile again from the beginning.
