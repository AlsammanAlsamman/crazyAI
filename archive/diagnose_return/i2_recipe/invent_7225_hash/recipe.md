CHOSEN SEED: Each mark is poured into the sickened wine cup and the cup's changed color becomes the seed for pouring the next mark, so the whole pile passes as one unbroken pour.

1. Lay the pile of marks out in a single row on the tavern bench, left to right, in exactly the order they were given to you. Do not sort them, do not group them, do not set any aside. Count them and chalk the count at the end of the row, then lay one final mark after the chalk that says nothing but that count — this is the tail mark, and it is now part of the pile.

2. Set out the cup. Fill it not with pure wine but with wine already cut — the tavern's sickened draught, whose color is fixed and known to every taster in the house, the same for me and for a stranger in another town. This starting color is the collar color. Write it down; you will need to prove later you began from it and not from a color of your own choosing.

3. Take the leftmost mark. Pour it into the cup. Stir once with the breath held — one continuous motion, no pause — so the mark's weight and the cup's standing color mix into a single new color. This is a pull.

4. Taste the cup and read its color. Do not write it down as a step of its own; instead let that color become the color the cup now stands at, and let it be the color that receives the next pull. The cup is never emptied and never refilled. The color you just read is the only thing that carries forward; the mark itself is discarded off the bench once poured.

5. Move to the next mark on the right and repeat steps 3 and 4 exactly as before — same cup, same single held-breath stir, same tasting. Do this once per mark, marching rightward, never skipping, never going back, never pouring a mark twice, never pouring two marks together. The pile passes as one unbroken pour from first mark to tail mark.

6. When the tail mark has been poured and tasted, stop pouring. Do not stir again. The cup now stands at its final color, and that color is the cup's whole memory of the pile.

7. Carry the final color out of the tavern and through your own organs: present it to each of seven organs in turn, in fixed order, always the same order. Each organ bends the color against the color it was handed, and each throws away half of what it received — keeping only the half that refuses to sit still — and hands that surviving half to the next organ. No organ may be skipped and none may be visited twice.

8. Take what the seventh organ hands back. This is the token. Knot it into a jacket collar so it holds one size only — the same small size for a pile of three marks and a pile of ten thousand.

9. Test it at the garden door. Reach back into the pile as it was, change exactly one mark — choose the quietest one, the one you'd least expect to matter — and pour the whole pile through from step 2 again, from the collar color, in full, all marks, all seven organs.

10. Hold the new token beside the old one at the nightingale's note. Count how many of their threads differ. If about half the threads differ and no run of threads sits unchanged in both, they have traded beauty for beauty and the method stands.

11. If fewer than half differ, or any recognizable shape of the old token survives in the new, throw the whole method away — return to step 2 and use a more sickened wine, or a harder bend at each organ. A knot that remembers its old shape after one thread is pulled is no knot at all.

12. You are finished when three things hold at once: any two different piles you pour end at two different tokens; the same pile poured twice by two different hands from the same collar color ends at the same token; and every one-mark change you try in step 9 passes the test at step 10. Then the pour is sound, and the token is the pile's true name.
