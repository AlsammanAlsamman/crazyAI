Looking at this honestly — three of the four layers map cleanly onto known, checkable problems. One (retroactive functionalization) is well-trodden ground (it's just exaptation), so I won't pretend it's a fresh application on its own; it's more useful stacked with the others.

**1. Side-channel leakage in cryptographic hardware — the strongest match, covers layers 1–4 together.**
Layer 1: a probe (power meter, EM antenna, microphone) is grafted onto a chip that's running for an unrelated purpose (computing a cipher), not built to inform anyone. Layer 2: the leaked signal isn't a direct read of the secret key — it's current draw or EM emission (a proxy for switching activity), which is itself a downstream effect of the hidden variable (which key bit is being processed). Layer 3: the decode rule ("this trace shape = bit 1") is discovered empirically by an attacker correlating traces against known outputs (differential power analysis / template attacks), not specified by the chip designer — meaning assigned after the fact by whoever watches the correlations. Layer 4: the standard defense (masking/blinding — the "ritual counter-action") introduces its own independent hazard: masked implementations are measurably vulnerable to *higher-order* attacks, and that added leakage is worst exactly around the most sensitive operations, i.e., risk compounds at peak stakes rather than being neutralized.
Check: this isn't speculative — it's the literature on DPA and higher-order masking (Kocher et al. on DPA; Coron/Prouff on higher-order masking attacks). You could verify the compounding claim concretely by measuring whether a masked AES implementation's leakage-detection metric (e.g., TVLA t-statistic) actually rises under higher-order analysis relative to the unmasked baseline.

**2. Structural health monitoring via vibration/strain proxies — covers layers 1–2 plus the missing-ground-truth problem.**
You can't directly observe fatigue cracking inside a bridge or turbine blade, so you instrument something already vibrating for unrelated reasons (normal load-bearing operation) and read off a derivative — natural frequency shift, strain-gauge rate — that's a proxy of a proxy for the real hidden condition. Communities of engineers calibrate "this frequency drop = this much damage" by convention/history, often without a real feedback loop to ground truth (destructive testing is rare and expensive). The known failure mode matches the passage precisely: temperature and humidity shift the same frequency signature that damage shifts, so the proxy is confounded exactly like "slow=calm, fast=claimed" has no way to distinguish "claimed" from some other cause of stutter.
Check: this is directly testable against the SHM literature (e.g., Sohn's environmental-variability reviews) — pull a dataset with both vibration signatures and independently verified damage states (lab fatigue-test benchmarks like the Los Alamos SHM datasets exist for this) and see whether temperature-normalization is required to avoid false positives, which would confirm the "uncalibrated proxy-of-proxy" diagnosis.

**3. Automated safety responses whose corrective action carries its own hazard, worst at peak alert — covers layer 4 specifically.**
This is the MCAS/737 MAX pattern (and similar stall-warning-triggered nose-down corrections): a sensor proxy (angle-of-attack) triggers a prescribed automated counter-action, and that action is itself dangerous if the underlying signal was wrong or the crew is already in a high-stress, low-margin moment — so the "cure" and the "disease" become correlated in exactly when they're most fragile, rather than the cure mitigating risk uniformly.
Check: this is checkable against incident/accident investigation reports (NTSB, aviation safety databases, or hospital alarm-fatigue incident logs) by testing whether secondary-intervention error rates rise specifically during high-urgency/high-workload episodes rather than staying flat — that's the discriminating prediction the structure makes (compounding at peak alert, not just constant background risk).
