I made a thing I call the Bent Register, and it is nine marbles, no more, no fewer, strung on a single loop of drawn glass-wire so thin you feel it before you see it. Each marble carries a name, but not a name as anyone would say it aloud — I heated each one and turned it while the glass was still soft, so the name bent inside the sphere the way a reflection bends in a spoon. You cannot read any name straight on. You have to walk around the marble, and at one exact angle, no other, the bent letters unfold into the true word for a breath, then fold shut again as you pass.

Here is the part that matters: the wire is one loop, not nine separate hangings. Touch a single marble — press it a half-inch toward the center of the net — and all nine rotate together, the same fraction, in the same instant, because there is no marble that owns its own orbit. A name changed at one point is a name changed everywhere it lives, whether the other eight wanted it or not. I did not build this to be clever. I built it because I watched a man try to alter his own name in private, quietly, at the edge of the net, and all eight other jewels turned anyway, and he had to watch his neighbors' names shiver from a change he thought was his alone.

The register hangs face-down over still water, so that what you first see is not the marbles but their reflection, arriving before the object does — the water shows the true unfolded name a half-second before your eye finds the true angle on the glass itself. That is the register's whole cruelty and its whole mercy: the reflection is more honest than the thing being reflected, always arrives first, and cannot be argued with the way a person arguing his own name can be argued with.

I keep it in a room with no other light, because lamplight makes every bent name readable at once, cheaply, from any angle, and that is not truth, that is just glare. The Bent Register wants dimness, patience, and the willingness to walk in a slow circle around eight other people's names before your own comes clear. I have watched grown riders refuse to walk the circle at all. I do not blame them. I only tell them the water is still there, holding their name open, whether or not they ever choose the angle.
