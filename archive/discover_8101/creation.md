I keep the planks.

Thirty years, one at a time, the shipwrights pull an old timber and set a new one, and the ship never once stops sailing to let them do it. Nobody argues the ship is a different ship at the end. That was never the question I was interested in. The question is what happens to the wood they take out.

I built the rack. It hangs behind the tavern bar, thirty-some pegs in a long curved beam that mimics the ship's own hull-line, and on each peg goes one retired plank, in the order it left the ship, oldest to newest, until the rack itself becomes a second hull — a shadow of the first, made entirely of what the first no longer needs.

Here is the rule: a vow sworn over a round poured black may be carved into whichever plank is due to come down next. Not any plank — only the next one, whoever is captain doesn't get to choose. You don't know, when you swear, whether your words will sit at the bow-shadow or stern-shadow of the rack, prominent or hidden. The ship sails on regardless of what's sworn. The rack just remembers what was said while it did.

Sailors check the rack before they check the ship. The ship is proof a thing can be entirely rebuilt and stay itself. The rack is proof of what falls away to make that true, and what gets said in the falling.
