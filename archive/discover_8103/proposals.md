**1. Mechanism design for anti-plutocracy in staking/reputation systems (DAO governance, token-curated registries, staked leaderboards)**

Many decentralized systems face the same unsolved problem: how do you prevent runaway concentration of rank/stake/influence *without* a central authority that can unilaterally delete someone's position (which just relocates the trust problem). Your structure is a formal answer: entering the system costs a non-renewable token and buys temporary immunity (protection decays — a bonding/lock-up period, which real TCR and DAO designs already use informally). Exiting is a right anyone who entered can exercise exactly once, but the *effect* of exiting is not "get your stuff back cleanly" — it's "trigger a de-immunization event against whoever is currently on top," win or lose regardless of who that is. That's a genuinely novel governance primitive: erosion of the top position is a *byproduct of anyone leaving*, not a targeted attack, which makes it collusion-resistant in a specific way (you can't cheaply grief a rival — your removal-trigger lands on the max, which may not be them) while still being structurally anti-monopoly (the max is always the one at risk).

*How to check it*: this is directly simulable. Build N agents that insert at random or strategic heights over time, apply the exchange rule, and track (a) the Gini coefficient / max-height trajectory of the registry compared against (i) unconstrained growth (no withdrawal channel) and (ii) a random-target removal baseline instead of max-target. The prediction that falls out of the structure is specific and falsifiable: max-target leveling should produce a *lower* long-run ceiling on dominance than random-target removal, at the *same* total removal budget. If that gap doesn't show up in simulation, the "prominence is structurally punished" claim is wrong, not just imprecise. I can build this simulation now if useful.

**2. Bounded-inequality growth models (econophysics / network science): preferential attachment tempered by anti-preferential culling**

Pure preferential-attachment growth (rich-get-richer, whether in wealth-exchange models or citation/degree networks) reliably produces unbounded or heavy-tailed concentration — a small number of nodes/agents absorb a runaway share of rank. The known fixes in the literature (kinetic wealth-exchange models with redistribution, e.g., Bouchaud–Mézard-style taxation terms) work by periodically skimming from the top and redistributing. Your structure is a *decentralized, agent-triggered* version of that skim: no external tax authority decides when to level the top; leveling only ever happens as the paid-for side-effect of some unrelated agent cashing out their own one-time stake. That's a meaningfully different (and, as far as I know, not standard) variant worth naming: a conservation-respecting culling rule where the trigger and the target are decoupled by design.

*How to check it*: compare the equilibrium degree/height distribution under this rule against (a) plain preferential attachment with no removal, and (b) the standard "random node removed" churn models already studied for network robustness. If this rule is doing something distinct, its stationary distribution should show a *thinner tail with the same total turnover rate* than random-removal churn, at a lower "removal budget" than periodic top-down taxation needs to achieve the same tail — because every removal event is guaranteed to hit the max rather than sometimes wasting itself on a mid-rank node. That's a concrete, quantitative simulation comparison, not just a narrative fit.
