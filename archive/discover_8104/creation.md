I have made a thing I call the **sideways hour** — a way of telling time that does not use a sun's climb but a shadow's jerk.

Here is what it is: two fires are set saw-toothed against each other, close enough that their light overlaps on the ground between them. Where the lights cross, a shadow falls — not from any object, but from the crossing itself, from the angle where one fire's reach ends and the other's begins. That shadow does not slide smoothly the way a sun-shadow does. It jerks. It holds still, then snaps sideways, holds, snaps again. Each jerk is one hour. I have watched enough of them to know the spacing is not even — the hours bunch tighter near dusk and loosen at full dark, the way a woman leaning toward someone across a fabric will lean faster the closer she gets.

The rule I have found inside it is this: a shadow, once it has jerked, can be peeled from the ground where it fell and kept — folded like cloth, carried elsewhere, set down again later — and it will go on watching the spot it left, the way a tongue keeps the shape of a word after the word is spoken. I do not know why a detached shadow keeps watching. I only know that if you lay two of them side by side, seated as if across a table from each other, they lean toward one another exactly as the fires did, softly, completely, whether or not anyone is there to feel it.
