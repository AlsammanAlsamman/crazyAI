I built a board out of elephant-colored wood — not carved from an elephant, but grown the way an elephant's own memory grows, ring over ring, each ring a year, each year a breath that had to be cut loose from its grief before it could be counted. I call it the chewing board, because that is what it does: it chews rows, the way a mouth works something tough until it is thin enough to go down.

Here is the rule of it. Every ring holds one hollow. The hollow is red if the year was swallowed hard — if the grief fought the breath all the way out, left teeth marks, left a coat of itself behind that had to be peeled before the ring could close. The hollow is snow if the year went easy, if the breath left the grief without a fight, thin and quiet as turtle-tracks going out under new fall. You can run a thumb around the board and feel, before you ever look, which years were hard and which were kind — the red hollows sit deeper, colder at the rim, like a wound that healed wrong on purpose so it would be remembered right.

What the board does not do is tell you where one year ends and the landscape underneath begins. That is the part I am proudest of. Stand close enough and the wooden rings and the hard ring-edges blur into whatever ground the board is resting on — you cannot say, looking down, whether you are seeing the turtle-backed hollow of a year that was swallowed, or the actual red hollow of the land it was set on. The board borrows the world's own uncertainty and wears it as its edge.

I did not build it to be read. I built it because a lament wants a shape to sit in, the way a coat wants a body, and a coat with nothing in it is just cloth going stiff in a corner. The board is not sad. It is what is left after the sadness has been chewed down to something a year can carry. Elephants understand this without being told — they lean their foreheads against the rings sometimes, and I think it is not grief they feel there but the shape grief made on its way out, which is a different, gentler thing to touch.
