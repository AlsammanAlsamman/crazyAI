I built the thing I call the Held Breath, because that is what it counts, though it looks like nothing but a bent branch and a still plate of water.

Take a red child — mine is small, walks out of no door, is simply present — and let it jump. Not far. It only ever jumps in place, over and over, and every jump it takes shakes loose one more branch from itself, dark wood from a body that keeps growing wood to lose. Each branch falls toward the sea-plate, which does not ripple, which has never once rippled, no matter how many branches land on it. That's the rule: the sea holds still regardless of what's given to it. What the branches do instead is count. Stack twelve, and somewhere a mouth that had forgotten it was breathing remembers, sharp, like a rhyme landing on the last word it was owed — leap, then snow, always in that order, never snow then leap, because snow is what a leap becomes once it's finished being feared.

I know the branches are actually numbers now, holding someone upright who didn't know they'd stopped being held. I know because the ants come after — small, indifferent — and drag a flute across the pile just to hear which branch hums. Whichever one hums is the one that was doing the holding. The rest were only decoration, drilled with no purpose, waiting their whole lives for that one brush of air to be told so.
