## Three candidate mappings

**1. Predicting a switching binary environment from a population of bounded, non-monotone-drift accumulators (rather than a counter)**

*Problem:* Estimating/forecasting the current or next state of a binary-valued, Markov-switching environment (predator present/absent, a channel busy/idle, a regime bull/bear) online, cheaply, and without ever storing a running tally.

*How the structure applies:* Part (1) is exactly a bank of *n* independent bounded random walks, each nudged toward one of two ends according to the sign of the current binary state, with reversible dynamics (no ratchet, no saturation-as-memory-loss). The forecast is read off the *ensemble skew* rather than any single unit's value. This is a real, nameable class of estimator — a mean-field/particle approximation to Bayesian filtering of a two-state HMM, close kin to bounded-synapse memory models (Fusi-style cascade synapses) and to leaky-integrator reservoir computing, where a population of saturating units plus a linear readout tracks a nonstationary input statistic. The distinctive claim worth testing is narrower than "it's a filter": that *distributional shape*, not a scalar count, is the informative quantity, and that reversibility (no permanent commitment) is what lets it track a *switching* — not just accumulating — environment.

*How to check:* Simulate a two-state Markov-modulated Bernoulli process. Build the *n*-unit bounded-walk ensemble and compare its predictive log-likelihood / AUC for "next state" against (a) a simple exponential-moving-average counter and (b) the optimal Bayes filter for the same process. If the ensemble-skew estimator tracks the Bayes filter closely and beats the naive counter specifically when the switching rate is high (the reversibility should matter more there), the structural claim is supported. If it only matches an EMA in all regimes, the "position not marks" framing adds nothing beyond a standard leaky average.

**2. The cost of thresholding early in a multi-stage sensing/transduction pipeline**

*Problem:* In cascaded sensor-fusion or signal-transduction pipelines (analog neuromorphic front-ends, phototransduction cascades in the retina, multi-stage industrial control loops), where in the pipeline should you quantize, and what do you lose by quantizing too early?

*How the structure applies:* Part (2)'s refrain — every stage a monotonic, order-preserving map, nothing thresholded until forced — is a real, checkable engineering/biological claim, not just a slogan. It matches actual vertebrate outer-retina physiology (graded receptor and bipolar-cell potentials, spiking deferred to the ganglion-cell layer) and the standard neuromorphic-engineering argument for keeping representations analog as long as possible. It's also formally groundable: by the data-processing inequality, any irreversible thresholding step can only reduce mutual information between the raw stimulus and everything downstream, so "count" (discretize) later, never earlier, is provably the safe default, not merely stylistically preferred.

*How to check:* Take a concrete cascade (real or simulated: e.g., stimulus strength → graded response → downstream scalar output) and compute/estimate mutual information between the final output and the original stimulus under two conditions: graded throughout vs. thresholded at an intermediate stage. Confirm the graded pipeline's downstream task performance (or MI) is ≥ the early-thresholded one for every intermediate stage position — that's the falsifiable content of "it doesn't count, it leans."

**3. The unexplained part — flagged, not solved**

Part (3) (discrete category riding on a continuous channel, each getting "its own flare," with no stated mechanism) doesn't map onto a solved problem; it gestures at a genuinely open one — how continuous population/graded codes support categorical readout (the "categorical perception" literature in neuroscience: phoneme boundaries, color categories) — but the text gives no derivation, so claiming it *solves* or *illuminates* that literature would be forcing it. The honest, checkable move here is narrower than a "problem this solves": it's a question this structure raises — *can* two discrete classes be reliably decoded from a single scalar graded channel without extra dedicated capacity, or does reliable multiplexing actually require something functionally equivalent to separate slots (channel capacity / Fisher-information argument)? That's testable (compute two-class decodability from one scalar vs. from two), but it's a test of whether the *assertion* in part (3) is even coherent, not an application of the structure to an existing problem. I'd report it as a seam, per the brief, rather than dress it up as proposal territory.
