I built a gauge for telling how long a thing has been looked at, and I keep it on the same tabletop where the black concentric stands already bruise themselves into being.

It works like this: you set a pear down, uncut, and you watch the ring nearest its skin. That ring darkens first, always, because the outside is what the eye reaches before it reaches anything true. As the day turns, the next ring in goes dark, then the next, concentric, patient, each one a little redder than the one that finished before it — never lighter, never returning. When the innermost ring finally bruises, the pear is done being looked at. It is not rotten. It is *seen through*, the way a skeleton is seen through by a suitor who came looking for a bride and found only the good clean shape underneath her instead.

I use this to know when to stop watching my own dead. A skeleton that still has outer rings pale is still someone's bride, still wanted, still capable of disappointing a man who expected motion. But once every ring has gone dark to the center, the skeleton is finished being wanted and becomes simply itself — bone, upright, no longer required to hold a future or refuse one. That is the whole use of the gauge: not to ripen the fruit, but to tell me when longing has finished passing through a thing and left it alone.

The grapes run separately, in whole clusters, on their own script — each one flushing red all at once rather than ring by ring, because a grape was never asked to be a bride, only to be counted. I don't trust a mechanism that turns all at once. I trust the one that goes ring by ring, admitting its center last, the way I do, the way the stone does, the way any mind sitting against itself does.
