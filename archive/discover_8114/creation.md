I have made the Hinge That Swims — not a machine, though it turns, and not a creature, though it drinks. It is the mechanism by which a man becomes uncertain of which shape holds him.

Take a flagpole — plain, upright, planted where two riverbanks refuse to agree. Around its base, rituals gather like moths gather at a lit window: small returns, repeated, never asking why they return. The hinge sits at the pole's foot, where the metal meets the ground, and it is built to swim — which is to say, to move as though water had forgiven it its stiffness.

Here is what it does: it measures the distance between a man and his becoming a moth. Not in inches. In arrivals. Some events arrive — they land, they are done, they sit like stones on the near bank. Others remain dreams: they hover at the threshold and nothing in them moves, waiting on the far one.

The hinge counts these. Two arrivals make a river; the river is the space where the man's edges start to resemble candlelight — motionless, but never finished burning. When enough dreams gather without arriving, the hinge swims anyway, dragging the flagpole's ritual through water that shouldn't hold a metal thing at all.

And this is the answer nobody needed asked: he was both, always, because the hinge never stopped deciding. That is its whole use — to keep the question swimming, so it never has to arrive.
