# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

In a big meadow there is a home burrow where the Queen Mouse lives, and dozens of other burrows scattered around, all linked together by a tangle of tunnels. Every tunnel takes a certain number of seconds to crawl through — some are short and quick, some are long and slow, but none of them ever helps a mouse go *backward* in time (no tunnel ever "un-crawls" you faster than not crawling at all).

The Queen Mouse wants to send a cheese-crumb message out from her burrow, and she wants to know, for *every single burrow in the meadow*, the fewest seconds it could possibly take for the crumb to arrive there, relayed mouse to mouse through the tunnels.

Here's the catch that makes the mice grumble: the rule everyone's been taught is that before any mouse is allowed to poke its nose into the tunnels leading *out* of its burrow, that burrow's own arrival time has to be completely locked in, official, stamped, done — no take-backs. Only then can its outgoing tunnels be checked. And to figure out which burrow gets locked in next, someone has to stop and compare the current best-guess time of *every single unlocked burrow* in the whole meadow before picking the smallest one. Every burrow in the whole meadow has to go through this locking process, one at a time, before the Queen can say for certain how fast the crumb reaches even her own next-door neighbor's burrow.

Some of the younger mice find this maddening — they're standing right at a burrow with a perfectly good tunnel in front of them, itching to scurry down it, and they're told to wait because the burrow "isn't locked yet." Is that waiting actually necessary, or just what everyone's always done?

# SOLUTION 1

Round by round, the Queen keeps a scrap of parchment with a rough guess for how many seconds it'd take the crumb to reach every burrow (starting at "no time at all" for her own burrow, and "who knows, ages" for everywhere else). Each round, she sends a runner to physically visit every single unlocked burrow, read off its current guessed time, and remember whichever one has the smallest guess. That burrow gets stamped "locked" — its time is now final. Then, and only then, mice crawl out through every tunnel leading from that freshly-locked burrow, and for each burrow at the far end, they check: does going through here beat that burrow's current guess? If so, they scratch out the old guess and write a smaller one. Then the whole visit-everyone-and-find-the-smallest process starts over, again checking every remaining unlocked burrow from scratch. This repeats, one burrow locked per round, until every burrow in the meadow is locked.

# SOLUTION 2

Instead of sending a runner to re-check every unlocked burrow from scratch each round, the mice keep a single sorted line of ticket-holders waiting outside the Queen's den — one ticket per burrow, each ticket showing that burrow's current best guess, and the line is always kept in order from smallest guess to largest. Whenever a guess gets improved for some burrow, its ticket gets pulled out and slotted back into the line in its new, better position. To find the next burrow to lock in, nobody needs to walk the whole meadow — they just take the ticket at the very front of the line, since it's already sorted. That burrow gets locked, its tunnels get checked, any improved guesses get their tickets re-slotted into the line, and the mouse at the front of the line is pulled again. This gets the exact same locking order as Solution 1, just without the tiring meadow-wide search each time.

# SOLUTION 3

Forget locking burrows in order entirely. Instead, all the mice at all the burrows act at once, in repeated waves: in each wave, every mouse looks down every tunnel leading out of its own burrow — locked or not, doesn't matter — and shouts across, "if you came through me, you could get there in this many seconds total, is that better than what you've got?" Any burrow that hears a better number than its current guess writes it down. Nobody stamps anything as official during the wave. After a wave finishes, everyone does another wave, and another, checking all tunnels from all burrows over and over, because a burrow's guess might still get beaten by a sneakier, longer-looking path that turns out to add up to less. Once a whole wave goes by where not a single burrow's guess changes anywhere in the meadow, the mice know nothing more can possibly improve, and every burrow's number is quietly treated as final — without anyone ever having declared, one at a time, "this burrow, and only this burrow, is now locked."
