# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

Granny Pell keeps one jar on her belt as she walks the length of her flower field, west end to east end, visiting every flower exactly once, no doubling back, no skipping ahead — and she has to reach the east gate before the market opens, so she can't dawdle.

At each flower, she dips a tiny spoon into that flower's nectar and pours it into the jar. Before she's allowed to take a single step toward the next flower, she must give the jar one hard, deliberate figure-eight twist-stir — not a gentle swirl, a real full twist, wrist and all — so the new nectar is properly worked into whatever's already in there. Only then does she move on.

By the east gate, the jar holds her "field blend" — one pot of honey that's supposed to stand for the whole walk.

Here's the rule the market judges care about: if even one flower along the row had been a different kind entirely (say a clover swapped for a thistle), Granny's final field blend should come out looking and tasting like a completely different pot of honey — not just "a bit off," but roughly half of its color, thickness, and scent should be unrecognizable from the original batch. A blend that barely changes when one flower changes is considered worthless, even if it was quick to make.

Granny always used just the one jar clipped to her belt, stirred in place, filling up a bit more with each flower. Some of the younger beekeepers in town figure: if one hard twist-stir per flower helps mix things, then giving the jar three or four twist-stirs per flower must mix it even better, so they've taken to twisting away like mad at every stop — though nobody's quite sure that's actually true, and it sure slows down the walk to the gate.

The trouble is, Granny's belt only has room for the one jar. Everything — every flower's nectar, all the way from the first to the last — has to get worked into that same single pot, in that same single place, the whole walk long. Some of the other beekeepers grumble that one jar seems like an odd thing to insist on: a single pot can only hold so much texture before new nectar just sort of dissolves into what's already a fairly settled color, and a lone beekeeper twisting one jar can only be in one place doing one thing at a time. They wonder whether the field blend might come out livelier — or the walk go faster — if the nectar weren't all funneled into that one jar at all.

# SOLUTION 1

Keep the one jar on the belt for the whole walk, just like Granny does. At every flower: dip the spoon, pour the nectar in, then give the jar one firm figure-eight twist-stir followed by a quick flip of the jar upside-down and back (to make sure nothing settles to the bottom in the same pattern every time). Then walk to the next flower and repeat. Never stop to re-stir an earlier addition, never skip a flower, never go back — one pass, west to east, one jar the entire way. The strength is in doing that one twist-stir *every single time*, without fail, so no flower's nectar ever slides in unmixed.

# SOLUTION 2

Instead of one jar, Granny clips four small jars to her belt — A, B, C, D. As she walks the row, she pours each flower's nectar into just one of the four jars, taking turns in order (flower 1 into A, flower 2 into B, flower 3 into C, flower 4 into D, flower 5 back into A, and so on around again). Each time a jar gets a new pour, it gets its own firm twist-stir right then, same as before — but the other three jars sit untouched at that moment. She still walks the row only once, start to end. Only at the east gate, once every flower has been visited, does she pour all four small jars together into one big jar and give that combined jar one final hard twist-stir to make the field blend.

# SOLUTION 3

Granny doesn't carry a jar at all while walking the row. Instead, she and three helpers walk the row together and each dip a small cup at every other flower, so pairs of neighboring flowers each end up sharing one little cup, twist-stirred together right there. Once the whole row has been walked this way, they're left with a row of small cups instead of individual flowers. They then pair up *those* cups two at a time, pour each pair together into a slightly bigger cup, and twist-stir again. They keep doing this — pairing up whatever cups remain and combining them, one round after another — fewer and fewer cups each round, until only a single cup is left. That last cup is the field blend.
