# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

Grandma Nell makes a "locker spinner" charm for each of her grandkids. Every morning she reaches into a bag and pulls out a handful of colored pebbles, one at a time, in whatever order they come out. She has to turn those pebbles into a single decorated spinner wheel with painted blades, and the pattern of blade colors is the "secret" that only that kid's locker will open to.

Two rules matter most to her:

1. If you swap out even one pebble for a different color, the finished spinner should end up looking almost completely different — roughly half the blades should change color. That way nobody could peek at one pebble and guess the rest.
2. She only has one spinner, and she wants to finish before the school bus comes, so whatever she does with each pebble has to be quick.

The way Grandma Nell was taught as a girl: take the pebbles strictly in the order they come out of the bag. For each pebble, before you're allowed to touch the next one, you must give the spinner a "special twist" — you spin it forward a number of turns worked out by combining the pebble's own color-number with however many turns the spinner is already carrying, multiplying those two numbers together — and only after that twist do you press the pebble's color into the next blade. She was always told this multiplied twist is the *only* way to really scramble the pattern, and that if you're ever worried the mix isn't good enough, the fix is simply to run the whole bag through a second or third time for extra twists.

Her grandkids want to know: is there another way to get the same "one pebble changes everything" result, without needing that special multiplied twist every single time, and maybe without needing to wait for pebbles one-by-one at all?

# SOLUTION 1

Do it exactly the way Grandma Nell was taught. Keep one spinner. Take the pebbles strictly in the order they were pulled from the bag. For each pebble: look at its color-number and the number of turns the spinner already has, multiply those two together, spin the spinner forward that many turns, then press the pebble's color onto the next blade. Only once that's done do you reach for the next pebble. If the final pattern still looks too similar to yesterday's, run the same bag through the same steps a second time before calling it finished.

# SOLUTION 2

Keep one spinner and still take the pebbles in order, but drop the multiplied twist entirely. For each pebble: instead of working out a multiplied number of turns, just flip the spinner over (swap its left half and its right half) and give it one plain quarter-turn — always the same simple flip, no matter what the pebble's color is. Then press the pebble's color onto the next blade, overlaying it on whatever color is already sitting there rather than painting over it cleanly. Move to the next pebble and repeat the same flip-and-overlay. No twisting device, no working out products of numbers — just a fixed flip and a color overlay, every single time, and the pattern still ends up unrecognizable after a swapped pebble.

# SOLUTION 3

Don't insist on one spinner or one strict order at all. Pour the pebbles out and split them into pairs. Grab a friend or two, and each pair of hands takes two pebbles and presses their colors together onto a small scrap spinner — just an overlay, nothing multiplied. Now you have half as many little spinners as you started with pebbles. Pair those little spinners up the same way, pressing their patterns together in pairs again, and keep going — pairing and pressing, pairing and pressing — like a bracket at a school tournament, until only one spinner is left. Everybody can work on their pairs at the same time instead of waiting in line, it finishes faster than doing pebbles one after another, and swapping one pebble's color still changes about half the final spinner, because that change rides along through every pairing it touches on its way to the top.
