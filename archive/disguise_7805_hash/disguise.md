# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

Down at the boardwalk, Nadia runs a custom taffy stand. Customers hand her an order card listing a sequence of flavor drops — say, cherry, then lime, then licorice, then blueberry, and so on, sometimes twenty drops long. She starts with one plain lump of taffy and pours the drops onto it one at a time, in the exact order written on the card, folding and pulling the taffy after each drop before she pours the next one.

Two things matter to her:

First, the finished taffy's swirl pattern has to work like a signature. If two customers hand her cards that are identical except one drop is swapped (lime instead of lemon, say), the two finished taffies must come out looking completely different — a wild, unpredictable difference in the swirl, not just a faint smudge in one corner. That way nobody can fake an order, and nobody can look at two taffies and guess they came from almost-the-same card.

Second, she's got a whole line of customers waiting, so she can't spend all afternoon folding one taffy. Whatever she does between drops has to be quick.

Her rule so far: pour a drop, fold/pull the taffy a set way to work it in, then pour the next drop — never two drops at once, never skip ahead, never split off a second lump. One lump, one drop at a time, start of the card to the end.

Her old mentor always told her: "When in doubt, just fold it more times. Extra folds never hurt — more folding always means more mixed, more unique-looking taffy." But her new helper watched a few batches and noticed something odd: past a certain point, extra folding doesn't make the swirl more special at all — it makes every taffy, no matter what was poured into it, slump into the same dull muddy brown. The colors don't get more wildly scrambled with more folding; they just get averaged away into sameness. So "more folding = better" quietly stops being true, and might even work against her — she needs the swirl to stay distinctive and fast to make, not just endlessly re-folded.

# SOLUTION 1

Keep the single lump, but make each fold count for more instead of doing more of them. Every time a drop is poured, Nadia does one deliberate, strong twist-and-pull — not a gentle stir, but a specific corkscrew motion that drags color from the top of the lump all the way through to the bottom and back, crossing it over itself. Because the twist is strong and specific rather than repeated many times, one twist per drop is enough to smear that drop's color across the whole lump before the next drop goes on. She never touches a drop twice, never adds a second lump, and never repeats the twist "for good measure" — she just makes sure the one twist she does is a thorough, corkscrewing one instead of a weak, lazy one.

# SOLUTION 2

Instead of working one single lump the whole way through, Nadia splits the order card into a few short chunks — say, every five drops becomes its own small taffy ball. She works on all the small balls, folding each one just enough to mix in its own few drops, and she can even hand one ball to her helper so two get worked on at the same time. Only at the very end does she take all the small, already-mixed balls and braid them together into one final lump with a single big twist. The signature-like unpredictability doesn't come from repeating the same fold over and over on one lump — it comes from that final braiding step scrambling all the small, already-distinct balls together at once. This also happens to be faster, since the small balls can be worked on side by side instead of waiting in a single line.

# SOLUTION 3

Nadia stops treating every fold the same way. Instead of always doing the identical twist after every drop, she alternates between a few different hand motions — twist after the first drop, then a stretch-and-fold after the second, then a braid-over after the third, cycling through her small set of different motions as she goes down the card. Each motion disturbs the taffy in a different way than the others, so drops don't just get "folded in more," they get folded in *differently* depending on where they fall in line. Because the motions vary instead of repeating, she doesn't need to do any of them more than once per drop to get a thoroughly scrambled result — variety does the work that extra repetition was supposed to do, and it doesn't slump into that same muddy sameness no matter how far down the card she goes.
