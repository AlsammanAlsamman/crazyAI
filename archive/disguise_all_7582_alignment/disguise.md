# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

Two campers, Mira and Theo, each spend a whole afternoon leaving a trail of painted footprints across a very long stretch of sidewalk chalk squares — one square painted after another, all the way from a starting line to a finish line far away. Both trails have exactly the same number of squares. Each footprint is painted one of four colors.

The camp judges want a single official "how alike are these two trails" score, using the same rulebook every year so results are always comparable: matching colors at the same stop earn a point, a mismatch loses a point, and either camper is allowed to have quietly hopped over one of their own squares partway along (leaving a gap in their trail) to better line things up with the other trail later — but a hop costs two points.

The rulebook's required method is this: build one giant scoreboard, with Mira's footprints listed down the side and Theo's listed across the top, so every square of Mira's trail is checked against every square of Theo's trail, not just the ones that happen to land at the same spot. You fill the scoreboard one little box at a time, always starting at the top-left corner and moving box by box, and you're never allowed to write a number in a box until you've already written the numbers in the box directly above it, the box directly to its left, and the box diagonally above-left of it — because whether a hop was worth taking here depends on already knowing how things stood one step back. Both trails must be walked in the order the campers actually laid them down, start to finish — never starting from the finish line or reading a trail backwards.

For two short trails this is a fun afternoon activity. But the camp has started having contestants lay down trails hundreds of yards long, and doing this one box at a time, always waiting on the three neighboring boxes, with a single judge working through the whole giant scoreboard alone, takes forever — even though most of that scoreboard turns out to hold nothing interesting, and an awful lot of far-apart boxes plainly have no business depending on each other in that strict a chain.

# SOLUTION 1

Do exactly what the rulebook describes, literally. Get a huge sheet of graph paper, write Mira's colors down the side and Theo's colors across the top, and start in the top-left corner. For every single box, look at the score already written in the box above, the score in the box to the left, and the score in the box diagonally above-left; work out from those three neighbors and the colors being compared whether it's better to call this a match, a mismatch, or a hop, and write the resulting number in the box. Move to the next box to the right, then the next, finishing a whole row before dropping to the start of the next row down. Keep going until every box in the giant sheet is filled. The number in the very last box, at the bottom-right corner, is the official score. It's guaranteed correct because it follows the rulebook to the letter, but with hundreds of yards of footprints on each trail, one judge filling one box at a time takes an enormous amount of time.

# SOLUTION 2

Notice that since both trails are the same length, the two campers almost certainly stayed roughly side by side the whole way — nobody wanders off wildly to one side for that long without it being an obviously terrible trail. So instead of drawing the entire giant scoreboard, only draw a narrow diagonal ribbon of boxes running from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner: the boxes where Mira's footprint number and Theo's footprint number are close to each other, plus a modest cushion of extra boxes on either side to allow for a reasonable number of hops. Fill in that ribbon the same box-by-box way, using the same three neighboring boxes each time, but skip drawing and filling in all the far-flung boxes that compare a footprint near Mira's starting line to one near Theo's finish line — those would only matter if the trails were wildly out of step, which a quick look already tells you they aren't. This gets the same final answer almost every time, using a tiny fraction of the boxes, unless the two trails are unusually strange, in which case the ribbon needs to be widened.

# SOLUTION 3

Bring a whole team of judges instead of one. Notice that any two boxes lying on the same slanting line running from lower-left to upper-right of the scoreboard never actually need each other's numbers — each only needs boxes from the slanting line just before it. So instead of one judge crawling along one box at a time, line up a row of judges along that diagonal, hand each one a different box on it, and let them all fill their boxes at the same moment, since none of them is waiting on any of the others. Once that whole diagonal line of boxes is done, shift the whole team one step over to the next diagonal line and have them all fill those boxes together, and so on, sweeping the team of judges diagonally across the scoreboard from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner. Every box still gets filled using the same neighbors as before, in the same order relative to each other, and the final answer in the bottom-right box is identical to Solution 1 — it just gets there far quicker because many boxes that don't depend on one another get done in the same breath instead of one after another.
