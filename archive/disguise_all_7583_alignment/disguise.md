# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

Two lines of children stand in a school parade, front to back, and both lines have exactly the same number of children. Every child in each line is holding up one colored flag — red, blue, green, or yellow.

A judge wants to score how alike the two lines are. Walking to the front of both lines together and comparing the child at the very front of Line A with the child at the very front of Line B, then the second child with the second child, and so on to the back, is the natural way to check whether they're "in step." When a child's flag matches the child across from them, the pair earns a point. When the flags are different colors, the pair loses a point. Sometimes, though, one line has a child who stepped out to tie a shoelace, which shifted everyone behind them back by one spot — call this a "slip." A slip costs even more than a mismatch, but it might be worth it if it lets the rest of the two lines snap back into step afterward.

The judge's rulebook, though, doesn't let her simply walk both lines together. It insists she first build one giant scoreboard — a big chart with a box for every child in Line A crossed against every child in Line B, not just the ones standing directly across from each other. So there's a box for "first child in Line A versus first child in Line B," but also a box for "first child in Line A versus the very last child in Line B," and every other far-flung pairing in between, even ones nobody would ever seriously consider a sensible match. The rulebook says every single box in that entire chart must be filled in, one at a time, and that each box's number depends on the box directly above it, the box directly to its left, and the box diagonally above-left of it — so you can't fill in a box until those three neighbors are already done. A slip's value can only be discovered by first knowing the score for everything that came right before it. And both lines are always walked from front to back, never back to front.

Only after the whole chart — corner to corner, every Line-A child against every Line-B child — is completely filled in does the rulebook let the judge read off, from the last box, how alike the two lines really are.

# SOLUTION 1

Do exactly what the rulebook says. Draw the full scoreboard: one row for every child in Line A, one column for every child in Line B. Starting from the front corner, fill in every box, going row by row, left to right. For each box, look at the three boxes that already have numbers in them — the one above, the one to the left, and the one diagonally above-left — and decide which of three things is best so far: matching the two flags at this box (add a point if they're the same color, subtract a point if not, then add whatever the diagonal box already had), taking a slip by dropping down from the box above (subtract a bigger penalty, then add whatever that box had), or taking a slip by coming in from the box on the left (same penalty, from that box's number). Write the best of those three choices into the box, and move on to the next one. Keep going, box after box, row after row, until every box in the entire chart is filled — including all the far-corner ones nobody would sensibly use — and then read the answer out of the very last box, in the bottom corner.

# SOLUTION 2

Notice that a shoelace slip only ever nudges a line a tiny bit out of step — nobody drifts wildly far from their original spot in line just from stopping to tie one shoe. So instead of drawing the whole giant scoreboard, only draw a narrow strip of boxes running along the natural front-to-back matching — comparing each Line-A child only to the handful of Line-B children standing near roughly the same spot in line, and skip filling in any of the boxes way out in the far corners, since no reasonable amount of slipping would ever call for using them. Fill in just that narrow strip the same box-by-box way as before, then read the answer from the end of the strip. Far less scoreboard to draw, and the answer comes out the same as long as no child ever drifts further out of step than the strip allows for.

# SOLUTION 3

Skip drawing any scoreboard at all. Walk down both lines together, one step at a time, front to back, keeping only a single running tally in your head instead of a written chart. At each step, compare the two flags directly in front of you and adjust the tally up or down. Keep in your head just enough of the last few steps' worth of tallies — not the whole history, just a slim moving window of it — so that if it looks like a slip might have helped somewhere recently, you can check by glancing back only that short distance, decide, adjust the tally, and then let that older memory drop away as you keep walking forward. By the time you reach the back of both lines, the running tally in your head is the final score, and no giant chart was ever built or kept around.
