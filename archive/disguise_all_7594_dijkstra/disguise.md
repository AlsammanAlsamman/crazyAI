# TRANSFORMED PROBLEM

In the middle of Bramblewood Forest stands the Grand Oak, and Squirrel Mimi lives there. All around the forest are other hollow trees, and old rope bridges connect some of the trees to others — never all of them to all of them, just certain pairs. Every bridge has a crossing time painted on a little sign at its start (some bridges are quick wobbly planks, some are long and slow), and crossing a bridge never makes you arrive earlier than if you hadn't crossed it — a bridge can slow you down or leave you the same, but it can never magically save you time.

Mimi wants to know, for every single hollow tree in the forest, the fastest possible time to get there starting from the Grand Oak, hopping across however many bridges it takes.

Here's the part that bugs her assistant, a woodpecker named Gus, who's in charge of making the announcements official: the forest council has a rule that a bridge's crossing time may only be "counted" toward figuring out a neighboring tree's fastest time once the tree at the start of that bridge has been formally and permanently declared "settled" — meaning the council is totally sure no faster way to reach it will ever turn up, and no more updates to its number are allowed. Until a tree gets that official stamp, none of its bridges may be used to update anybody else's fastest-time guess, even if a squirrel already has a very good, very believable guess sitting right there.

Gus grumbles about this constantly. Scouts keep scampering back with fresh timing reports — "hey, I found we can reach Willow Hollow in 6 minutes, not 9!" — and Gus has to say "sorry, can't tell Willow Hollow's neighbors yet, Willow Hollow isn't settled." Meanwhile the council also insists that to pick which tree gets settled next, someone has to walk the entire list of not-yet-settled trees and compare every single one's current best guess, every single round — even trees clear on the far side of the forest that obviously won't be settled for ages. And technically, to be fully sure about the fastest time to any one particular hollow, the council wants the whole forest checked and cross-checked before anyone signs off on a single number.

Mimi just wants correct fastest-times to every hollow, worked out as quickly as possible — and she's starting to wonder whether Gus's "settle it first, only then tell the neighbors" rule is really necessary at all, or just forest bureaucracy.

# SOLUTION 1

Mimi keeps a big chalkboard with one line per tree: "best time found so far." The Grand Oak starts at zero, every other tree starts blank ("no time found yet"). Each round, Mimi walks past every single line on the chalkboard, from top to bottom, and picks out whichever unsettled tree currently has the smallest number written next to it. She puts a gold star next to that tree's name — settled, forever, no more changes. Only now does she go check every bridge leading out of that tree, and for each one she asks "does crossing this bridge, using the settled tree's official time, beat the number currently written for the tree on the other end?" If yes, she erases the old number and writes the better one. Then she starts the next round: walk the whole chalkboard again, find the next smallest un-starred number, star it, check its bridges. She keeps doing full walks of the whole chalkboard, round after round, until every tree has a gold star. It works and gives the right answer, but on a forest with hundreds of trees, walking the entire chalkboard every single round is a lot of extra legwork, most of which turns up nothing new.

# SOLUTION 2

Mimi recruits a helper, a magpie named Pip, who's very good at organizing shiny objects by size. Instead of a plain chalkboard, Mimi gives Pip a set of nesting boxes arranged so the box with the smallest number is always sitting right on top, instantly grabbable — no walking the whole collection needed. Whenever a tree's best-time number changes, Mimi just tells Pip, and Pip slides that tree's marker to its correct spot among the boxes in a few quick moves, without disturbing everything else. Now, each round, Mimi just asks Pip "what's on top?" — gets the answer immediately — stars that tree, settled, and checks its bridges exactly as before, updating any neighbor's number and telling Pip to re-sort just that one marker. Same rule about only using a settled tree's bridges, same correct answer at the end, but no more long walks down the whole chalkboard every round — just a quick tap on Pip's shoulder each time.

# SOLUTION 3

Mimi decides to test whether Gus's "settled first" rule actually matters. She tells every scout: the moment you hear of a better time to reach the tree you're standing at — official or not, gold star or not — immediately run across every bridge leaving that tree and tell the tree on the other end, "hey, here's a possible new best time for you, update your guess if it's smaller than what you've got." No waiting for permission, no checking for stars. Since no bridge ever saves time (only adds it or does nothing), a tree's number can only ever get smaller or stay put as reports come in — it never needs to be walked back up. So the scouts just keep gossiping updates back and forth, tree to tree, bridge to bridge, and whenever a tree receives a report that doesn't beat its current number, that scout simply stops and doesn't bother passing it further. Eventually, no scout has anything new to say anywhere in the forest, and every tree's number is quietly, correctly final — without a single formal "settling" ceremony ever happening. It turns out the star and the announcement rule weren't required for the right answer at all — just for the council's peace of mind that nothing would be double-checked out of order.
