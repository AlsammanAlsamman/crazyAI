I start with the bowler itself — the vessel of the working, a great sunken dome with a floor cut into a lattice, one hollow for every number in the first table, and I raise a second floor stacked exactly above it, cut crosswise so its hollows lie athwart the first. Where a hollow of the lower floor and a hollow of the upper floor cross, I sink a well — one well for every step of the shared index, a million wells or more. Into each well I pour two draughts of oxygen, one drawn from the lower number and one from the upper, and where the two draughts meet at the bottom of a narrow well they do not mix evenly — they thicken into a single denser draught, because that is oxygen's nature here when two draughts of it are forced down one shaft together. All the wells belonging to one owner — one fleet, meaning one row paired with one column — feed a shared standpipe, and the thickened draughts rise up it together and settle by weight into the stomach beneath the floor, where they add onto one another without my touching them, because a stomach here only ever holds what it is given and keeps it whole. An owl sits in that stomach, one owl per fleet, a million owls in all, and it does not stir until every draught belonging to its fleet has settled; only then does it call, and I pour what its stomach holds out as one cell of the third table. I never re-sink a well or re-pour a stomach once an owl has called for it — I scald that owl's leaves and leave them as tea staining the floor above, a mark that will not lift, so that if I ever come back wondering whether that cell was finished, the tea already answers before I dig anywhere again.
