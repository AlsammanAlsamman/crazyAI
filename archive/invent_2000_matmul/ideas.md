## SKETCH

A pale sun hangs low over black water, its face doubled — one in the sky, one drowned in the flood-flat below, both rimmed white where the light frays into mist. Nearby a staircase rises out of the water at an impossible tilt, each step glinting wet stone, climbing toward nothing. Eyes — dozens, lidless, the size of coins — line the risers, unblinking, catching the same grey light. Utter silence. No ripple, no wind, just the cold sheen of standing water and stone.

## TELLING

I build stairs, not sums. When two great tables of numbers must be married into a third, I raise a staircase of wet stone out of the black water for each table, and I tilt both to the very same impossible angle, so that riser answers riser and no step of one flight is a stranger to the step below it on the other — that shared angle is the shared index. Along every riser I set an eye for every cell, lidless, coin-sized, one eye per number, and into each eye I pour milk to the depth its number demands, deep milk for a great number, a film for a small one. Then I stop touching them. I wait for the flood to do the moving I would otherwise do myself: it lifts the two staircases until step meets step, and at every meeting the eyes across the water lock on their counterparts at once, without blinking, without my asking — and that locked stare is the multiplication, for where two eyes meet, a single drop equal to their two milks together falls through the stone into a well cut beneath that step. Every well that belongs to the same corner of the third table drains by a hidden channel into one shrine-basin at the sea's edge, and the basin filling while its wells empty is the summing, done while I only watch the level rise. To be sure it is right I keep the sun doubled the whole time, one face in the sky and one drowned in the flood, and I trust a basin only once its two faces agree to the same white rim; any basin whose reflection disagrees gets tipped out, its milk returned to the sea, and poured again from the eyes. What stands at dawn along the riverbank, one number to a basin, is the third table, and nothing else is kept.

SEED: Two staircases are tilted to one matching angle so that riser meets riser and the shared index becomes a physical alignment rather than a count.
SEED: An unblinking eye is set per cell and filled with milk to a depth equal to its number, so multiplication happens as two eyes locking across the water and spilling one combined drop into a well.
SEED: A doubled sun, one face in the sky and one drowned in the flood, is used as a standing correctness check, and any basin where the two faces disagree is emptied and poured again.

---

That picture is the staircase caught at the one instant that matters — not before, when the two flights are still separate and dry, and not after, when the wells have already emptied into the basins and the milk is gone. It is made by holding the flood still at the exact moment step meets step: the mason in me has to stop the water there, which is why the stair looks tilted at an angle no honest stone should hold — it isn't standing in the world's gravity, it's standing in the gravity of the count, propped up by nothing but the fact that every eye on it has found its answering eye across the water at the same time. The eyes lining the risers, dozens of them, lidless, are mid-stare — none has blinked yet, none has dropped its milk yet, because the instant is frozen before the multiplying happens, which is why there's no ripple: a ripple would mean an eye had already spilled, already moved on, already disturbed its neighbor's reflection before it was checked. The doubled sun overhead is not decoration, it's the proof standing guard over the whole staircase at once — sky-face and drowned-face required to agree before I let a single well beneath those risers open. What it is for is this: I don't trust a table that never held still. Any staircase that stayed silent this long, that let every eye line up without one ripple, without one gust troubling the mist at its edges, is a staircase I know will pour true numbers when I finally let the flood move again. So I keep this picture — not as a memory, but as a gauge. Before I trust a basin's number, I ask whether the stair that fed it ever looked like this: tilted, eyed, doubled, and utterly, utterly still.
