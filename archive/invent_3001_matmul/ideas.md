I take the two great tables into the store-room and I do not touch them cell by cell — that way madness lies, and eyes ruined by sunlight learn that fast. Instead I hang them as two painted canvases on facing walls, each canvas ruled into the same fine grid of a thousand-by-thousand squares, and I let the window's light fall through one onto the other. The trick is in the tortoise: I ask her to walk the length of one wall at the pace a shadow moves, and wherever her shell's edge crosses a painted square on the first canvas and a painted square on the second at the same instant, the two grey values burn together — not added by hand, but smoked into a third canvas hung between them, cigar-ash settling into whichever square the light-and-shadow crossing points to. I do this in strips, not the whole million at once — I roll down a shade over all but one broad band of the grid, so the tortoise only has one band's worth of crossings to manage before I roll the next band into place. This is the only way the boiler-mind doesn't overheat: it never holds more than a strip's worth of squares awake at once, and the spring stays loose. What stays still: the canvases themselves, pinned flat, never re-hung mid-work, because a canvas that shifts even a hair's width during the burning throws every sum into the next square over and ruins the count. What moves: the light, the tortoise, and the shade. What I wait for: the ash to settle fully in a strip before I roll the shade further — I've learned that impatience here means smoke drifts and a square gets someone else's ash. What I throw away: the used light itself, spent and grey, swept out the window after each strip, because light that's already crossed a square once will lie if you ask it to cross again. When every strip has smoked its ash into the third canvas, I take it down, hold it against the window, and check that the picture and the view align precisely — if the store-room's own remembered light matches what's painted, the sums are true, and I know it not by checking numbers but by the tower outside looking legible through the canvas rather than doubled or smeared.
