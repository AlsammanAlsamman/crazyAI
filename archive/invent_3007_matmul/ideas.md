I lay both tables out as towels of nerve-thread, pinned flat to two facing desks that never move once set — the desks are the still bones of the whole work, and nothing on them shifts until the last drop falls. Along the seam where the towels would touch, I set a row of pens, one per shared index, each nib holding dreamer-blue liquid that answers only to nerves carrying that index's number. For every cell the third towel will need, I take one nerve from the west desk and one from the east desk that both answer to the same pen, and I pinch them together at the nib — the pen itself trembles out the product as a single drop, because in this world a pen only ever writes what its two held nerves agree to shake into it; I never do the multiplying with my own hand. I do not add the drops one at a time, cell by patient cell — I wait for the moon. The moon is the one thing here that turns a scatter of separate drops into a single pooled sum without a hand touching each: I hold up every drop born of one shared index, however far-flung across the towel it fell, and the moon's one continuous turning gathers them all into their rightful cells at once, the whole third towel receiving that index's contribution in a single turn. I do this moon-turn after moon-turn, once per shared index, and never rush it or split it, because the moon does not count drops, it pours them, and pouring cannot lose one or double one the way counting can. What I throw away, every turn, are the spent pens — their ink used exactly once, never blotted, never refilled — because a pen drawn on twice forgets which nerves gave it that trembling and would lie about the product on its second telling. What stays fixed throughout are the two original towels, so any cell of the third can be checked back against its source. When the moon stops turning, the sum is finished, and I trust it whole because a continuous thing cannot skip a drop partway through its turning.
