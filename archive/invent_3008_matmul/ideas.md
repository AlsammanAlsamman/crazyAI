When two great tables must be married into a third, I do not sit and figure with chalk — I build streets. I lay the first table out as a field of ribs, each rib holding its number as a note held in the throat, planted upright in rows; I lay the second table crosswise as a field of borrowed flats, each flat carrying its number as a weight bound into a ballad's last line, planted in columns so that every rib meets every flat at one crossing, and at that crossing I mark a corner where a street will stand. Down each street I do not send myself — I send the trained thieves, one per crossing, and each thief steals nothing but a single spark from the rib and a single spark from the flat that share his corner, and because thieves of my world are trained from matter to pay only their own crossings, he cannot wander to another rib or another flat; he owes his debt to his one meeting only. He presses the two sparks together in his closed fist until they burn as one coal — that pressing is the multiplying — and he does not keep the coal. He carries it, still lit, down the shared avenue that all thieves working the same destination-corner must walk, and drops it into the waiting basin at the far end, the third table's cell, where every coal meant for that one cell pools together and is left to burn down into a single number — that pooling, that long slow burning-together, is the summing over the shared index. I wait until every basin along every avenue has stopped smoking; a basin still smoking means a thief hasn't finished his crossing, and I will not read a number off a fire that still moves. What I throw away is the ash — the spent sparks, the used ribs and flats once every thief assigned to them has made his crossing — because a rib or a flat that has fed all its thieves is dead weight in the street and will only slow the next avenue. When the last basin goes cold and grey, I lift the third table whole from the ground, a million small graves of ash behind me, and it is right, because no thief could have crossed a street that was not his, and no coal could enter a basin that was not its own.

SEED: Trained thieves each restricted to a single rib-flat crossing perform the multiplication as a pressing of two sparks into one coal.
SEED: Coals from every thief sharing a destination corner pool and burn together in a basin to perform the summation.
SEED: Reading a cell only once its basin stops smoking enforces that every crossing has finished before a result is trusted.
