I take the first table and stand its rows up as a long avenue of stone houses running east–west, each house's door bearing its number carved by the mason who lives inside it; I take the second table and plant its columns as a second avenue running north–south along the far bank of the mirror-lake, so the two avenues meet nowhere except in the water between them. For every house in the first avenue and every house in the second, I let their reflections fall onto the lake at once — the whole million of them shivering on the surface together, never one drawn before another, the way spheres passing through our flat world used to swell and shrink as circles without anyone timing them. Where a reflection from the east crosses a reflection from the south, that crossing is the shared index, and I read the two numbers waiting there and let them draw together the way neighbouring suns draw each other, the pull between them exactly their product, no more, no less. I keep a scratch-tablet by the shore, the kind that forgets everything the instant a cloud crosses the sun, and onto it I chalk each pull as it happens, orbit by orbit, the way electrons keep their circuits round a nucleus without ever missing a lap; when all the orbits for one crossing are spent, I add the chalk-marks into a single sum and cut that sum, permanently, into the new house of the third table, standing where the avenues would have met on dry land. Then I wipe the scratch-tablet bare — chalk is cheap, stone is not — and move to the next crossing, thirty thousand crossings, a hundred thousand, until the whole new village stands built. I trust none of it to arithmetic alone; I check it by walking the finished village at dusk and seeing, in the mirror-lake, that it throws back exactly the reflection the two avenues always meant to become, no throne, no king, only the sum itself.
