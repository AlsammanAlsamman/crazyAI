I do it in the desert, where the first table stands as rows of coin-stones sunk knee-deep in the ground — one stone per cell, its number stamped into the coin's face — and the second table hangs above it as a lattice of funnels swaying from ropes strung between two tent-poles, each funnel's throat marked with its own number. I never walk a single stone against a single funnel one at a time; a million cells would eat a lifetime that way. Instead I call the mason and we cut the whole field into marble tiles, sun-sized blocks of maybe forty stones by forty funnels, and we drag one tile of coin-stones under one tile of funnels at a time, so a manageable stampede of numbers meets another manageable stampede, and the ground under my feet never has to hold more than it can bear. For every stone that sits beneath a funnel, I pour a measured cup of sand through — the funnel's throat-width narrows or widens the flow to match the stone's stamp, so the sand that lands in the waiting bowl below is the product of the two, no more counted than that. The librarian stands at each bowl with her ledger open, and she does not wait for all the sand in the world to fall before writing; she adds each new fall to the running mark already on her page, so the bowl's total grows the way dust settles on a windowsill, a little at a time, across every tile we drag through, until every shared index has passed and paid its due. When a tile is spent — its stones weighed, its funnels emptied — I have the stampede haul it off to the shade and it never returns; I keep only the bowls, because the coin-stones and funnels themselves are chest-junk once counted, worth nothing more than the number they already gave up. What's left when the last tile has crossed is a third field of bowls, each holding its own settled sand, and I have the mason weigh every bowl once, exactly once, and stamp that weight onto a fresh coin — that coin becomes the corresponding stone in the new table. I never trust a single weighing twice, and I never let the wind near an open bowl before it's stamped, because a mismeasured grain of sand is a wrong answer wearing an honest coin's face, and a battle like this is only won by throwing away everything counted and keeping only what was written down.
