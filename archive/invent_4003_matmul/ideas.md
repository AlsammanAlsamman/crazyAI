I carry the two tables to the reed bed at the marsh's frayed edge, where every cell has already been cut into a pipe — its length and bore giving it one note, sure as a number is sure. For each cell of the third table, I lay the row-reeds of the first table beside the column-reeds of the second, one row and one column bundled into a single sheaf standing for the shared index. I bring the sheaf to the owl-headed woman's desk, where her triangular prism sits catching whatever amber light the desert throws off, and I feed the paired reeds through it two at a time. She never speaks; she only draws. For every pair, her bow crosses both reeds at once and a finch peels wet off the page, its wingbeat holding the product of that pair the way a shadow holds a shape. I never keep the finches separate — that would be a table nobody asked for. I let them flock, right there over the sheaf, and a flock does what flocks do: it becomes one voice. The single cry that comes out the far side of that flocking — the note all the birds converge to — is the sum across the shared index, and I write it into the matching cell of the third table before the ink dries. Then I release the flock through the window into the amber nowhere, because a finch that has already sung its number is dead weight, and the prism must stay clear for the next sheaf. A million cells means a million sheaves, but the marsh has reeds enough, and she never tires of drawing.
