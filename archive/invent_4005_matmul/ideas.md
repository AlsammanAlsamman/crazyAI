I take the first great table and lay its rows along the shelves of one room, each cell a pile of pebbles set at its own rung; the second table I lay along the shelves of the room across the hall, its rows turned crosswise so column meets row at the doorway between them. Over every doorway I hang the tied mirror, and where two piles catch its face at once it shows not two piles but their product, one heap where there were two, and I speak that heap's count aloud until it holds fixed. I never carry pebbles myself; the cat does, walking the spine of the book that never closes, dropping each product heap into the nest waiting on the bed, and only when every doorway along that shared rung — the whole index — has poured into the same nest do I call it done and press it flat into one cell of the third table. Since there are a million cells to fill, I do not tend one nest at a time: I open every doorway in the apartment together, let a hundred mirrors catch their hundred pairs in the same breath, and set a hundred cats running at once, for the wires behind the walls never tangle no matter how many rooms turn together. What I throw away is the tied mirror's face the instant its heap is named — a mirror that kept yesterday's reflection would lie about today's — and I throw away any pebble that fell outside its own rung, since a stray counts for nothing. I wait only for the slowest nest, never for the mirror.
