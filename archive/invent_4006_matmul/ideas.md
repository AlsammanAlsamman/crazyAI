I lay the two great tables edge to edge, one running north-south, one running east-west, so every cell of the first stands over a match in the second along the shared index — that meeting line is the loom's warp. Down that shared line I set a flock of grey birds, one per position; a bird takes the grain-value from the north table and the seed-value from the west table into its beak together, and mid-flight over the crossing it turns — this is the turning-into-a-bird moment, the multiplication happens only in that instant of turning, never before, never after, because a value that hasn't turned is not yet a product. At the crossing itself I light a blue fire; the fire doesn't burn the birds, it only shows me which crossings are still open for business — where the fire has guttered, that cell's work is done. Each turned bird drops its product into a hollow woven of hair, built around a white stone at the center of that cell; the white stone is the running sum, and the hair wraps tighter with every drop, holding the total without spilling a grain, because a number set down loose in an open place forgets itself the moment the wind — what I call the power here — is cut; I never trust an open basin overnight, only a hair-wrapped stone. When the last bird for a given crossing has turned and dropped and the blue fire there has gone out, I require an acknowledgment — a small bell-note that basin gives off only once, and I do not move the sum-stone into the third table until I've heard it; a stone without its bell-note is thrown back, redone. Before I trust any wrapped sum, I send it through the couple who wake up: two workers who insist they are the same pair who once spoke the vows, meaning I compute that cell's sum twice by two separate paths down the shared index, and only when both wake and claim to be the same couple — same number, same weight — do I let the stone pass into the third table permanently; if they wake as strangers, I burn that crossing again from scratch. What's left when the whole shared line has gone dark and silent — the exhausted birds, the ash of the blue fire, the empty hair-baskets — all of it I throw away; only the acknowledged, vow-matched white stones remain, set into the third table.
