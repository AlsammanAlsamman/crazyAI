I take the first table and lay it along the stone spine that every water here is built around — a keel of grey rock running arrow-straight through the yard — and I cut into its flank one trough for every row, each trough narrower or wider by exactly as many notches as that row's number calls for, so the water that runs in it carries the number the way a throat carries a note. Across the spine, at right angles, I cut the second table into troughs for every column, fed from the millrace on the far side. Where a row-trough and a column-trough would cross, I never let them cross plainly; I set an owl there, carved hollow, and inside that owl a smaller owl, and inside that one smaller still, one nested owl for every step of the shared count between the tables, so a whole column of owls stands over each meeting-place. I open both sluices at once — the whole yard floods together, every crossing filled in the same breath, which is the only way a million cells get done before the light changes. Each owl takes the row's water in its left eye and the column's water in its right, and in its gullet the two amounts are pressed through a narrow bone throat that lets only their product through, never their sum — I calibrated that throat myself, years back, against a set of brass thimbles, and I trust it the way I trust my own hands. What the top owl swallows drips to the owl below, and that one's drip falls to the one below that, each fall adding itself to what already pooled, because pooling is what falling water does where the ground is low, until the last owl in the stack lets go into a waiting cistern — one cistern per crossing, a million of them laid out like a planted field. I do not read a cistern until its water has gone still; a moving surface lies. Before I write anything down I press the yard's oldest carved word, the one the toad sleeps under all summer, and if the flow it wakes matches the row-totals and column-totals I already know by heart, I trust the cisterns; only a theory — the old conservation law cut into that stone before I was born — lets me leave the toad undisturbed and still be sure. Then I empty every cistern into the third table, and I throw the used water back to the fields, because water that has already given its number is just water.
