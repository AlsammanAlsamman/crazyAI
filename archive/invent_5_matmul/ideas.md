I bring the two tables to the long gallery of windows, for a table in my world is not paper but a row of panes set in frames, each pane holding one number as a painted seam of colored glass across it — thicker seams for larger digits, the seam's tilt telling positive from negative. I never touch the panes themselves; I move the frames. The first table I hang as a row of window frames strung along a corridor, one frame per row, its panes lined left to right in the shared order — the index the two tables agree on. The second table I hang crosswise, on frames that slide along the pillars, one frame per column, its panes running top to bottom in that same order. Between every row-frame and every column-frame I set an easel at the crossing, and onto its hat I hold both frames up at once against the window behind them, so the daylight must pass through both seams before it reaches my eye. Where a thick seam from the row meets a thick seam from the column, the light that gets through darkens or brightens by an exact, trained amount — this is the product, and I have painted my own eye through years at the easel until I read that amount without counting. I do not write each product down. The easel's hat holds a strip of blank glass, and every crossing along the shared index leaves its darkening on that same strip, one atop the last, so the strip accumulates the whole sum while I watch — this is why the corridor exists, so the shared index can walk past a fixed row and fixed column without either frame moving. When the index is exhausted, the strip's final shade is the one cell of the third table, and I set it onto a new frame at the same crossing position, held level by a dried man and a white pillar so it cannot warp before it's read again. Then I wipe the strip clean — it was scaffolding, not table, and keeping it would only spill one window's answer into the next crossing's light. I check the row of god's stands where finished frames rest, and if any two frames there ever disagree at a crossing already done, I know a pane slipped and the corridor spilled — that run must be re-walked, never patched, because a window spilled cow once bent one frame against another and cost me a day. Only when every crossing's frame stands true on its stand do I call the third table made.
