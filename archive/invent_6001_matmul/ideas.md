I keep no number as one clean mark. A quantity in my hands is a string of knotted cord, each knot a bead strung at its place — ones-knot nearest my palm, then the tens-knot, then the hundreds-knot further up the cord — because no cord long enough to hold a great count could be tied in a single knot and still be read by eye or finger. So before two quantities ever meet, each is already many small marks laid side by side, waiting.

To combine two such cords I never trust one grand joining. I carry them to the altar-board set into the castle wall at the mouth of the hall, where every pairing of single knot against single knot — one through nine, against one through nine — has already been carved and wine-stained into the stone by masters long dead. I don't reckon knot-times-knot; I look it up, finger tracing to the carved cell, and lift off the answer whole. Each such lifted answer I lay down shifted to its proper place on a fresh strip of bark, and where two answers land on the same place I push a carry-stone one strip higher and let the rest settle — cruder, smaller acts, dozens of them, standing in for the one act of multiplying.

For the two great tables — each a wall of corded numbers a thousand knots by a thousand knots — I don't work cord against cord across the whole wall at once; the counting-deck of the ship isn't wide enough to hold that much bark and stone together. I cut each wall into holds, timber-sized blocks that fit the deck, and set a rower to each hold. A rower takes one strip of cords from the first wall and one column of cords from the second, walks them together along the shared index, knot against knot through the altar-board, shifts and carries as I taught, and pours each partial sum into a running ledger-cup lashed at the third wall's matching cell. The scratch bark, once its answer is poured in, I cast overboard into the river — nothing kept that isn't the total itself. Only when every rower's cup has received its last pour, and the tide of the shared index has run out, do I read the ledger-cups onto the third wall, cell by cell, and call the wall finished.
