I lay the two tables out as wire-frames, not as single marks — each cell on them is never one indivisible thing to me, it is a comb of pin-notches strung along a fixed rod, a little village of teeth where each tooth stands for a value one rung higher than its neighbor, the way a spine of vertebrae stands for a length. To combine them I don't touch cell against cell blind and fresh each time — I build first a product-village, a board of pegs where every notch-pattern from one rod has already been laid against every notch-pattern from the other and the answer pin set once and for all; after that, meeting is not reckoning, it is looking, finger finding the peg already standing. I take a block of rows from the first frame and a block of columns from the second — small enough that both fit under one hand without my eye wandering — and I hold that block still, wedged with a peg, while the other slides across it tooth by tooth, so each pin-notch does its looking-up many times before it is ever set down and forgotten. Under each crossing I keep one holding-peg, a single accumulator that does not move house until its whole shared index has passed beneath it — every partial answer gets driven into that one peg and only that one, never scattered to be gathered later, because a scattered sum is where a village of workers starts lying to each other about what they already added. When the shared index is exhausted I lift the holding-peg's final notch-count straight onto the third fabric, wipe the chalk-lines of the block clean, and slide the wedge to the next block, discarding every intermediate mark the moment its sum is written, keeping nothing that isn't still owed to the answer.
