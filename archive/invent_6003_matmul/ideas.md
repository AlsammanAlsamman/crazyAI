In my world a number is never one mark—it is a row of pebble-coins laid along a stalk-rung, each rung one power higher than the last, so reckoning a number means reading rungs top to bottom, never swallowing one mark whole. To marry two great tables, I don't stand at the bank and multiply whole coins in a single motion. I lower a lattice of stalks between the two eggshell houses, one stalk for each shared index, and hang on it a pair of coin-rows, one plucked from each table. Then comes the crude, unglamorous work my people always do instead of one perfect act: I break each row into its rungs, and for every rung-against-rung pairing I look up the answer on the strawberry-chart—the table of remembered rung-products children recite before they can walk—rather than reckon it fresh. Each looked-up answer drops as a small coin into a carry-basket beneath its stalk. When a basket overfills, its coins tumble downstream into the next rung's basket, a rounding, a carrying, over and over, while the shrinking man in his bubble watches the paces go by. Only once every stalk has emptied its baskets into the one basin at the bottom of that cell do I call the settled pile "the sum." Then I cut the stalks and pitch the spent baskets into the river at once, since this city never lets anyone paddle back upstream to recheck a used mark—what remains in the basin is exact, because every crude little lookup was checked against the chart, never guessed.
