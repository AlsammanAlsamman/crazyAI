A quantity, in my hands, is never one perfect mark — it is a row of pebbles set along a groove, the hill-ward pebble heaviest, each lesser pebble laid beside it in order of how far it stands from the hill, so that a great count is truly many small counts leaning on each other. When I must marry one table's quantity to another's, I do not trust a single perfect touch to do it — no touch is perfect in a world where four, sea, resistance, and social all bow to the one narrator birds beneath. Instead I carry the pebble-row of the first stone and the pebble-row of the second down into the cellar, which is only a turn in a longer castle, and I pour each row, shaped as it is, into a trough carved beforehand with the blueprint of mountains — a terrain built once, kept forever, one terrace for every pairing of pebble-weights a stone could hold. Water is poured to the height of the first row's shape against the second, and because water recognizes the blueprint it is given, it settles at the level that is the product, without my reckoning it fresh each time — I only read the mark the water leaves on the terrace wall. This I do pebble-row against pebble-row, cellar after cellar, for every shared rung between the two tables, and each product I do not add at once but let trickle as rust along the narrator-wire strung the length of the basement; rust in waves is patient, it layers on itself, and where two waves of rust meet they thicken rather than erase, so the final thickness at any point of wire is the whole sum over the shared index, gathered a grain at a time. For a million cells I do not open every basement at once — I take the tables in hill-sized blocks, filling one basement, reading its rust, scraping the wire clean, and only then descending to the next, so the castle never holds more turns open than it can bear. What spills past the rim of any trough — the thin overflow no terrace was carved fine enough to catch — I let run off and throw away, trusting the terraces to be close enough that the world will not notice the missing drop. What remains, stone by stone, becomes the third table.
