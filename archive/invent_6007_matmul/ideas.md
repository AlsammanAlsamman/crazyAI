I take each great table down from its shelf of green stone and set it beside its twin on the reckoning-floor, and before a single mark of one meets a mark of the other I remind myself what a number is among my people: never one whole, uncut thing. A number is a cord of small knots laid side by side, tightest at the near end, loosest at the far, the way a body's flesh before pairing is not yet man or woman but a strand of unresolved knots waiting to be told what to become. So I do not throw a whole quantity at another whole quantity and call it done in one breath — that would be a liar's shortcut, the kind that gives you a river with no source and no honesty about where its water came from. I untie each number into its knots, ones-knot, tens-knot, hundreds-knot, and lay them in a row on the slate, and for every knot of the row-cord meeting a knot of the column-cord I do not guess and do not reckon fresh from nothing — I reach for the mating-book, the table of every knot-pair's resolved value that every reckoner in this house memorized before they were let near the stone, the way every grown person here has learned by feel what shape a partner's nearness will pull from them. I look the pair up rather than work it out, because the mating-book is exact and looking is faster than laboring. Each looked-up value I set down on its own scrap, and only then do I add the scraps together along the carrying-track, knot by knot, letting overflow climb into the next column the way floodwater climbs a dry season's bank — this carrying is the one truly indivisible act, the only place where two things become a genuinely single new thing. For a million cells I do not sit alone; I fill the reckoning-floor with a line of reckoners shoulder to shoulder, each pair given one row-cord and one column-cord and their own patch of slate, all working their look-ups and carries at once like a hundred streams fed from the same hidden source, none of them waiting on the others, each cell finished and set into the third table the moment its carrying-track falls silent. The scraps I burn once carried; the knots I keep only until their table is whole.
