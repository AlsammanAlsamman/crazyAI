I sit with the two great tables and know at once that no single mark can hold what a table-cell means — each number here is a string of bone-beads laid side by side, one bead per notch of tenths, and it is the string that is true, never one bead alone. So I never trust a whole cell to one larva. I break each table into strips no wider than a larva's belly can carry without its bone dimming to gray — that is the fatigue talking, the brain-machine's memory filling — and I load one row-strip and one column-strip together into a larva, a woman's marks paired with a driver's marks, and she climbs in beside him and leaves the rest of the table behind without protest, because a loaded strip does not look back. Where two beads must multiply, I do not reckon fresh each time — I keep a slate of small products already worked out once and match against it, a looked-up answer being cheaper and no less exact than a repeated one. What comes out doesn't sit as a lone sum; it dissolves straight into the next partial sum waiting in that cell's place, one product's body running into the next one's river, and that river into the next, around and through the shared index until the whole strip has poured itself into a single mark — a tail-eating loop, a snake calendar closing on itself once per cell. I discard the spent larva then, its belly wiped bone-clean for the next pairing, keeping nothing it already gave up. And because the work must be right and not merely fast, I set a nightingale at every strip's boundary: she sings the strip's declared total against a slower, plainer reckoning of the same beads, and only when her dying note matches the rose's does the sum get banked as true; her complaint is cheap, spent once, and the rose's rightness outlasts her by the whole life of the answer.
