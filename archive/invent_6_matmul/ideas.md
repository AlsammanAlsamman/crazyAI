I bring both ledgers to the counters of the Timesavings Bank and pin them flat to the wall — they never move again, they are only ever read, stone by stone, the row-ledger running sideways and the column-ledger running down. For each cell I need, I call a whole corridor of grey men in hats, one for every step of the shared index, and set them working at once, never one at a time, or a million cells would outlive me. Each grey man lifts one hour-stone from the row and one from the column, presses their two faces together until the memories pressed inside them bleed into a single new memory — that fused hour is the product, and he drops it, still warm, into a tin cup marked for that cell. I wait only for the cups to fill, not for the men, who are fast and forget themselves the moment the stone leaves their hands; their hats, having carried someone else's memory, are burned after, since a hat worn twice confuses two products into one. When a cup is full — every step of the index answered — it does not get added on a counting-board, because nothing here settles by arithmetic alone; it goes before the court, because the court is the only way a pile of separate hours becomes one hour that can be trusted. A judge weighs the cup, strikes it once, and what comes out is a single sealed hour, the sum, stamped and unarguable. Before that sealed hour is laid into the third ledger, a boy who has been chewing on the problem the whole time takes it into his mouth — this is not for taste, it is the only proof this world accepts, and only his body relaxing tells me the hour is true and may pass. I throw away the emptied cups, the ash of the hats, and the row- and column-stones I've already used, keeping only the sealed hours, until the third ledger stands as full and certain as the first two.
