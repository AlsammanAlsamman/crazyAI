I take the two strings of symbols and press each one into the rim of a different melting pocket watch, the symbols standing where the hours used to be, and I set both watches on the same table-edge that is also the sleeping-cliff-face, so they melt at exactly the same unhurried rate and never lie to me about time passing faster for one than the other. I let them run down side by side, symbol against symbol, and wherever two drips land together on the tablecloth I call that agreement and let them harden into small clear beads, which I keep. But I know a crossing this long always carries one place where a step goes missing or gets added — where a woman becomes a river, a river becomes a doorway, a doorway becomes a vase, and no amount of staring un-confuses it — so I permit exactly one slip. At the point where the beads stop matching, I bring up the unhurried, courteous carriage that pulls up unannounced, and let it carry one string's remaining drips forward a single groove without touching the other, the way it carries a passenger nobody sent for. I try that single carriage-crossing at every branch of the dead tree the wax has dripped along, since each branch is a candidate for where the step was actually lost, and I keep only the branch where, after the one crossing, the beads downstream go back to landing together cleanly; the other branches I snap off and drop into the river of raspberry juice to be carried out of sight for good. What is left when all the wax has finished falling is a tally: the clear beads that matched on their own, minus one toll for the single carriage-crossing I had to allow, minus a smaller toll for every drip that still refuses to land beside its partner even after the slip. That final count, chalked on the vase standing in the doorway, is the number for how well the two strings agree — honest, because it has already paid the one fare for the one gap the crossing was ever allowed to have.
