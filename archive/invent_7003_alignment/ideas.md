I take the two strings out onto the flat bone-colored ground, under the light that comes from nowhere, and lay each one out as a row of eroded shapes — the small alphabet here is a handful of shard-forms, no two market days carve them quite the same, yet each day's shard answers to only one letter. I set the rows a hand's width apart and walk along both with a pair of hinge-calipers whose two legs can only step forward or, at one joint, let a single leg pause while the other steps alone — that pause is the slip, the place where one string may fall out of step with the other for a stretch before the calipers close again. At every stride I carry the caliper's reading down to the pond's edge, the one that always looks exactly as still after as before, and I drop one stone for each place where the two shards under the legs don't match, one stone for each place I let a leg pause. The pond answers each stone with a single, precise sound, no more, no less, and I count the sounds, not the ripples, because ripples lie and settle back into stillness while a sound, once heard, stays counted. I try every place the pause could fall — every door where one row might lag half a beat behind the other — the way the market tries every mood before the day closes on the one that stuck, and of all those attempts I keep only the walk that cost the fewest stones, throwing the rest into the sand where the desert takes them without a mark. What is left when I reach the last shard on both rows is the tally of sounds, and the number I hand back is the length of the row minus that tally — the higher it sits, the more the two strings agreed to be the same animal wearing two coats, one slipped a half-step out of the other's shadow.
