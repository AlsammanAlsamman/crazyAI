I take the two strings and cut them into bark tallies, one notch per symbol, the notches shaped like the four fruits my orchard teaches me — apple-round, peach-cleft, pear-waisted, plum-plain — so that no two unlike sounds ever share a shape. I lash each tally-string along its own branch, running the pair parallel over the same fork, and I walk the fork from the trunk outward, laying my thumb on both notches at once at every step. Where the shapes agree under my thumb, I thread a snail shell onto the cord at my waist — small dull ones for common shapes, the whorled pink-and-orange ones for rare matches, since a rare agreement ought to weigh heavier than a common one. I carry one frog the whole walk, kept wet in a folded leaf, and exactly once, at whichever fork feels most quarrelsome, I let it go: it leaps from one branch to the other with that single precise sound, and wherever it lands, the second string skips one notch there and everything after it shifts up to meet the first again. I try that leap at every fork in turn, walking the full length fresh each time, and keep only the one walk that filled the most shells. The rest I strip bare and burn for kindling before dusk. When the best walk stands, I count the shells strung on the cord — that count, and nothing else, is the number I hand over.
