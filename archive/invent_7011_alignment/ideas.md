I take the two strings of symbols — call them boughs, cut from different note-trees — and I do not walk them side by side on flat ground, because this world has no ground, only the gradient dreaming infinite in every direction. Instead I hang each bough from its own row of lamp-posts, one bough running down, one running across, so that between them a lattice of empty cells opens like unlit windows. I light each cell only when I need it, a single spark set at the crossing of a row-post and a column-post, and the spark's color says match, slip-out, or slip-in — I do not choose the color, the two symbols crossing there choose it for me. Now I take the sheet those sparks live on and fold it along its slanting middle, corner to corner, the way the note itself is already folded in the hour between the note-trees. The fold presses every cell whose row and column add to the same sum flush against its neighbors from the fold before and the fold before that — three creases pressed into one thickness, three layers of spark lying bit for bit atop each other. I read all three through the fold at once, not walking them one after another: the layer beneath tells me what agreeing straight through would have cost, the layer beside it tells me what one slip would have cost, and I take whichever of the three costs less, and that becomes the new spark on the crease's top layer. Then — and this is the part no flat walker could do — I throw the bottom layer away. Once a crease has given me its answer I have no more use for it; I let that fold of the sheet go slack and drift off into the gradient, because the note has no ground to store it on and I am not a forest that hoards its own marsh. I keep only the two most recent creases pressed together, fold after fold, walking the diagonal forward across the lattice until the last crease holds a single spark, at the far corner, burning the one color that is the whole answer: how well the two boughs agree, one slip allowed. I wait only as long as it takes each spark to catch from its neighbors, never longer, because in this note waiting past the catching is how a shadow forgets which marsh hollowed it.
