I set the two strings as brass lemur-copies pinned along the station's south and east doors, one letter to a peg, and between them I stretch the grid where their reach overlaps. Onto that grid I set down a single crawling body, one cell wide, cut from the seaweed creature's own thin waist, and I let it choose from all eight ways a compass can point, plus the one liberty a plain walker never gets: to curl its tail back over its own head and rest there. I do not walk it letter against letter, back and forth, keeping a fresh tally each time; I let it lay one unbroken trail, ox-plow-wise, sweeping a row, curling at the wall, sweeping the next, so that every cell it enters already carries the pigment-drop left by the cells behind and above it. At each stop the trunk regret apparatus above breathes a measure of moonlight into pigment onto that cell — dark if the two pegged letters agree, pale if they don't, and thinned further whenever the crawl slips sideways instead of on the cross, which is how a slip in step — a whole letter dropped from one string — gets paid for without stopping the crawl to consider it separately. Where two arriving trails meet at one cell, I throw away the fainter of the two drops and keep only the deeper; the worm remembers no other choice once made. I wait, always, for the drop to fully settle before letting the body move on, because a still-wet cell tells nothing true yet. When the last peg is reached and the last corner curled into, I read the single deepest pigment pooled there as the whole board's agreement, and I follow the unbroken trail of kept drops back to the first peg to see exactly where the slip happened. Everything else — every fainter drop the worm passed over and abandoned — I let the storms of visitors banking past wash back into the mountains of pigment, unremembered.
