I bring the owl-headed woman two ribbons of symbols, each rune drawn from the same small pot of colors, and she sets them either side of her endless corridor of doors — one ribbon nailed along the lintels going right, the other spooled down through the crowd of dead ancestors holding up the trunk, going down. I never build the whole crossing of the two; I do not paint a door for every place where a right-symbol meets a down-symbol, because most of that grid is bare corridor, stone the lemur never even sniffs at. I walk only where the two runes share a color: there I dip my brush and the stroke lifts off the paper as a living bird, feathers still wet, and settles on a rung of the corridor — that rung is a treasure, marked, kept. Every place where the colors differ I let stay empty stone and throw it from mind without a second look. Once the birds have landed, I don't send the creature down every corridor to find them — I send it hopping bird to bird, and it may only step to one further right and further down than the last, since no rung is higher than the one born after it. The green-faced man stands by the single cracked pillar and holds it steady through exactly one stumble — one place where the ribbons slip out of step, one treasure reached slightly out of turn — but a second crack and the roof comes down, and that whole chain is refused, thrown out with the stone. What remains, when the last bird has perched and the creature has stood on every treasure once, is the length of that living chain, feathers still lifting into melody, and that count, weighed against the length of both ribbons, is the number I hand back: how well the two agree.
