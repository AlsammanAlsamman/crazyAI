I lay the two skeins of glyph-beads (fish-mouths, bowler-hat men, diamond-scissors, rose-eyes — the four scale-marks of my alphabet) along two lengths of gut-string, one strung between my own open palms, the other between the palms of whoever brought me the need. I hold them taut over the river that is time, low enough that the current can just touch the lowest beads. Then, without looking down, I walk my hooking-fingers down both strings at once, bead against bead, and every place they agree I let a bead drop and be carried off downstream — one bank for each string, one loop of stones gathering in the shallows where the two banks meet. Where they disagree I hold my breath and do not drop the bead; it stays threaded, dead weight. Because either string may have slipped a step — a fish darted early, a bowler-hat man arrived a beat late — I do not only walk them straight through. At each place along the string I pinch a single bight into one side, a small dead loop the length of one bead, and try the walk again from there, once for every possible bight-point, like a boy sliding his chair forty-four times to catch the sunset from every angle. Each trial's dropped-stone count is left on the forest floor to rot into mulch — dead trials feeding nothing directly but softening the ground — except the one trial whose stone-pile in the shallows is tallest; that bight point is the true slip, and I cut the string there for good. The final number I give is the count of stones that reached the same bank in that one best trial, minus the beads still holding their dead weight — a single sum, dropped like a rose into flowing water for the asker to keep.
