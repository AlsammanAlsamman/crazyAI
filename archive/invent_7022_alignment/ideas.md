I lay the two strings of symbols as knot-cords along two edges of a sand-tray, one running north the horse's length, the other running east the mountain's width, so their crossing marks a lattice of furrow-points — each a place where a symbol from one cord faces a symbol from the other. Rather than sending a hooded memory to kneel at each crossing in turn, I wake a single ink-worm, one cell wide, a stitch cut loose from the verses, and set it at the tray's near corner. It can face any of the eight desert winds: it walks straight along a cord's grain when the symbols agree, slants across a furrow on the diagonal when they still agree well enough to spend a grain of sand on the difference, and crosses sideways, spending nothing, when one cord must be let to slip ahead of the other — the one permitted stumble, where step and step fall out of match. At every junction it settles, it drops a mound of sand to the height its path has earned, then reads what mound already sits there from an earlier pass. If its own tally beats the standing mound, it kicks the old one flat and leaves its own; if not, it doubles back — curling its own body across ground already crossed — to try the junction from another face. It keeps no ledger apart from the sand itself; a beaten mound is thrown away entirely. The worm is done only when it reaches the tray's far corner and no junction anywhere still holds a mound it could improve by returning to it. The height standing at that last corner is the agreement between the two strings, and the whole crawling, curling, doubling path it leaves behind — never lifted from the sand, never broken into separate glances — is the one continuous reading of the whole board.
