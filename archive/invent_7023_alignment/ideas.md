I take the two strings — not thread but the twisted grass-ropes we tie our counting-knots into, each knot dyed one of four berry-colors, since our alphabet is small: root-red, ash-grey, sky-blue, bone-white. I hang one rope along the east wall of the empty room where no tiles were ever laid, and stretch the other from the doorway across the floor at a slant, so that every knot of one crosses, in the mind's eye, every knot of the other — that crossing is the maze the question means. I do not mark the floor at every crossing. I let the cat in. The cat that no longer has anyone to answer it only sits on a crossing when the two knots there share a color — those are the treasures, the lit tiles, and there are few of them in a floor mostly empty corridor. I wait for the low sun to come through the one window at the hour it always comes, because sun striking two knots of the same dye throws one shadow instead of two, and that doubled shadow is how I find every treasure at once without walking the room knot by knot. Then the real work: I want the cheapest way to visit the treasures the cat has found, in the order the ropes give them, allowing the ropes to slip past each other once — one place where a knot on one rope waits idle while the other rope runs ahead a few knots before they fall back into step. Between one treasure-shadow and the next I never lay a marker on the bare floor between; the bare floor's cost is only ever how many knots forward on the wall-rope and how many knots forward on the floor-rope it takes to get there, and that I read straight off the two ropes' lengths, the way I'd read a distance by counting doorframes instead of pacing it. At each treasure I keep only the one running tally that got there cheapest — with the slip already spent, or not yet spent — and the moment a second tally arrives worse than the first, I let it drop, the way I let a bad thread-end fall to the floor and don't pick it back up. So no: the treasures alone don't excuse me from the corridor's cost, but they excuse me from ever standing in the corridor to learn it, since the corridor's cost was never a secret — only its length was, and the ropes already told me that.
