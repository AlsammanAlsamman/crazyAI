I take the two strings and give each its own furrow in the mud-brick floor, two rows of little cone-pawns and spool-pawns set nose to nose, house against house, along the same road of lines and dots that runs the length of my workroom wall. Each pawn wears a carved beast on its crown — lion, bird, elephant, lotus-bud — and that beast is its whole meaning; no two beasts are ever mistaken for one another even in poor lamplight. I set the first row flush against the road's marks and hold it still — it never moves again. Then I take up the small horse's head, mane frozen mid-toss, and walk it along the second row's houses, one house at a time, because that piece and only that piece is permitted to make a string slip: wherever I set it down, every pawn behind it in that row shuffles one house forward, opening a gap, so the row is tried against the fixed one both unslipped and slipped at every possible house in turn — the horse never rests until it has stood in each doorway once, and once more standing nowhere at all. At each such trial I walk the two furrows together house by house: where lion faces lion, or bird faces bird, I lay down a lotus petal, cool and easy; where the beasts disagree I tie a knot of red thread pulled from the laughing woman's ball and drop it in that house instead. The dark figure's flute keeps the only clock I trust for this — one low note per house — so I never count two houses on one breath or skip one in my hurry. When a trial furrow is finished I gather its knots into my fist and weigh the fist; the count is the whole judgment of that trial, nothing else about it matters. I set that knot-count beside the road, and when the horse has stood in every house and I have a count for each trial, I keep only the fist with the fewest knots and fling every other fist, thread and all, out to the fish that are always flying past the workroom eaves — they take the discarded counts and I never see them again. What is left in my one kept fist, knot for knot, is the number I carry back: how far the two strings truly disagree once the one permitted slip has been placed where it does the least harm.

SEED: A fixed row of beast-pawns and a second row that the horse's-head piece may slip forward at exactly one house along the shared road of lines and dots.
