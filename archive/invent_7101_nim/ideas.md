For each heap I light a candle and let it burn only as long as an apprentice can hold a severed page against the flame without the page catching — that length is my measure, and I notch the candle's wax at every point where the flame would double back on itself, upside-down against its own dusk, marking a place the way a tower counts its floors. Each notch is a stone of grass I set among embers, one row of stars per heap, one row of years beneath. Then I walk the rows together, heap against heap, and wherever two stars sit at the same height while a third also burns there, I call that lament truest and let it be — burn is burn, the music stays even. But wherever one row shows a star alone at some height, unmatched by any other heap's ember at that notch, I know the whole business is off true, and I take from that heap — grass and wax together — until every height holds no star or holds two, never one, so the soil of the count comes back level. If the rows already show no lone star anywhere, I let a reed bubble decide for me, plucking whatever amount keeps the last fish for my hand, and I throw the rest into the buried bubble where it never surfaces meanwhile. What stays still is the tower; what moves is only the wax.
