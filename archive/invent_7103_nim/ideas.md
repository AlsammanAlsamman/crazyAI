I kneel at the heaps with my notched ferry-rod — the one I cut for measuring crossings, not distance. Each heap I walk twice: once counting by the ones, once by the twos, once by the fours, laying a pebble face-up on the rod wherever a heap answers "yes" to that doubling, face-down wherever it answers "no." This is how a crossing is read in my world: not as a number but as a tally of yeses along the rod, the way a ferryman reads which shores are lit at dusk. Where a doubling-mark has an odd count of yeses standing on it across all the heaps, I know the crossing is not yet balanced, and I owe the water a debt. So I go to any heap that carries a yes at the highest unbalanced mark, and I throw enough of it into the dark carry-basket — gone, drowned, not returned — until that heap's own marks flip to match every unbalanced mark exactly once, evening the whole rod to all face-down. Then I sit still. I do not reach for the next heap; stillness is also a move here, and the other's turn is his to spend or waste. I wait for him to unbalance the rod again, as he must, and each time I re-read it and throw away just enough to make it even, until the last object is thrown and the crossing — and the game — is mine, because I was always the one who left the water level.
