The eyes are two blackberries, wet and watching, and it is by their light that I read the heaps, for each heap is a stall of stacked fruit, vegetable, wheat, and I kneel before it as a merchant kneels before the bride too lovely and too late. I take the count of each stall and fold it in my head the way dough is folded into halves and halves again, toward the mirror-lake, thirty folds deep if the pile demands it, marking every fold where one object refuses to split evenly — a lone berry left sitting undivided, a small wet eye of its own. I lay these fold-marks of every heap one atop the next, matching mark to mark the way the two hearts are joined by their single shared artery, and where two marks land on the same fold they cancel each other's pulse into stillness; whatever keeps beating after all that canceling is my answer. If nothing beats — if the shared artery lies quiet — I touch no heap with purpose, I take some small safe handful and let the day pass to the other bidder, trusting that a heap disturbed without a beating pulse only teaches the two cities to mistake each other's reflection for their own sought king. But when the artery does beat, I find the one stall whose fold-marks carry the highest still-pulsing mark, and from that heap alone I remove grain and fruit and vegetable in the exact number needed to silence the whole shared artery, never guessing, only subtracting until that heap's marks answer every other heap's marks like the thirty reflections finally showing calm water. What I take, I carry to the compost pit behind the stalls and it is gone from the market for good. What I do not touch stays exactly as it was, bruised and ripening, waiting. Then I wait too — for their hand disturbs their own reflection, and I kneel again to fold.
