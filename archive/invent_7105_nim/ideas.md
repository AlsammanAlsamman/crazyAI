I read the heaps the way the hooded figure reads a pattern: not as numbers but as pressures. Each heap is a cistern in my yard, valved in brass, and what stands in it is water that is also money that is also the current running under the market stalls — pull the plug and it drains, evaporates, and whoever takes the last drop wins the house. I bring the cloth. The girls who embroider at the long table have stitched, without knowing it, every quantity I will ever need: a crossed thread where a pipe is open, a bare gap where it is shut, doubling itself rank by rank down the hem the way pressure doubles down a manifold. I lay a swatch of that spilling cloth against each cistern's brass gauge, column by column, and where an odd number of gauges show a crossed thread in the same column, that column is unquiet — it hums, the way a bad joint hums just before a main bursts. I find the tallest cistern whose gauge disagrees in the highest unquiet column, and there I open the valve and let exactly enough run to mud to make every column agree again, all threads paired, all gauges even — then I stopper it and step back and touch nothing else, because touching a heap that already agrees is how you flood your own basement. The winged lemur-thing at my feet watches the run-off with its ears flat; it can smell an imbalance the way it smells rain three valleys off, and if my count is true it goes still, which is how I know I have not miscounted. Then I wait — I let the roaring in my own ears settle, and I let the other player touch whatever heap they like, take whatever they like, because whatever they take unbalances the columns again and hands the unquiet straight back to me. I never grab the last cistern greedily; I let the pattern tell me when it is empty of disagreement, and only then, when every gauge across every cistern pairs off into silence and one cistern alone still holds water, do I drain that one to the bottom myself and take the win as plainly as scooping the last puddle off a market floor after the flood has gone. What I throw away is any move that looks clever but leaves a column unquiet — I discard it the instant the girls' thread shows me the hum, the way you discard a debtor's contract that still hides an odd clause in the small print. The book stays shut once the pattern is memorized; a hooded figure only ever needs to read it once.
