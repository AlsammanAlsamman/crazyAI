I gather the heaps and carry them up into the tower, where the identical-faced girls wait at the long table with the cloth already spilling from its vessel. I do not give the girls sticks — I give each girl a heap to watch, and I set them stitching in rows: the first row is for one stick, the next for two, the next for four, the next for eight, each row a shell wider than the last, the way electrons keep their distance from the nucleus and from one another. Each girl stitches into a row only if her heap, broken into those doubling shells, has that shell lit. Threads from different girls cross in the same row without tangling — that is the whole point of a shared row, it belongs to no single heap. I walk the finished cloth top to bottom, hunting the highest row where an odd number of threads cross; a row where they pair off evenly I pass over, it needs nothing from me. When I find an odd row, I pick the one girl whose heap, if trimmed, would make every row from there down pair off evenly again — I unpick her threads down to that new count, and the sticks I removed from her heap I throw into the hotel room that always empties by noon, gone, not coming back. If I walk the whole cloth and every row already pairs off evenly, there is no such girl and no such thread to unpick, and I take some idle handful and wait for the foot soldiers' patient heart-bearer to move again, watching the cloth for the next uneven row to open.
