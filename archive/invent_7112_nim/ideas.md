I kneel before the heaps the way I kneel before deadwood on the forest floor, where fallen words lie waiting to feed the living ones, and I treat every heap's sticks the same way: not as a count but as hands wanting partners. For each heap I fold its sticks two by two, again and again, stacking pair upon pair upon pair — first-hands, second-hands, third-hands, each fold a coarser reach down that heap's arm. Wherever a fold leaves one stick with no partner, that heap's hand is unpaired at that reach, and I let the trouble slap sideways, exactly as the saw on my bench jerks sideways instead of down — from heap to heap, along the row, at that same reach only. A heap already unpaired there swallows the slap and goes quiet; a heap already paired there takes the slap and grows loud. Nothing about earlier turns or later turns crosses my mind — I hold no future in my hands, only the row as it stands. When every reach, checked in turn from the coarsest fold down to the plainest single stick, comes back quiet across the whole row, I know the row itself is what must move me next turn, not my will. But if some reach comes back loud, I find whichever heap is loud at the deepest, coarsest reach, and from that heap alone I remove sticks until every reach in the whole row folds quiet — the rest I throw on the forest floor to feed what comes after. That heap, touched once, correctly, is my move.
