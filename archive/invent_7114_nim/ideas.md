The heaps are brought to my bench and I weigh each one against my case of graduated limestone weights—one deben, two, four, eight, sixteen, each doubled from the one before, the same case the temple scribes true their scales with. For every heap I lay down the largest weight it will bear, then the next against what remains, until the whole heap is spoken as a row of small clay tallies, one pressed into a square of the senet board for each rank of weight, three rows of ten so no rank ever crowds another. Then I read straight down each column of squares: a column holding an odd number of tallies I touch with a pinch of red ochre, and it burns; a column holding an even number I leave as still stone. If nothing burns, the position has already turned against me, and I take but a single token from whichever heap loses least, then wait, hands folded, for the other player to disturb his own balance, as sooner or later he must. If a column burns, I find the heap whose tally lies in the highest burning column, sweep that heap's excess counters off the board into the archive's discard jar, and rebuild the heap smaller until every burning column, counted again, comes up even. The unburning columns I never touch—that stillness is the whole of my patience. Then I close my hand and wait for his move; whatever heap he lifts from, I return to the weights and mend the columns again. The last object is always mine, because I never let the ochre keep burning past my turn.
