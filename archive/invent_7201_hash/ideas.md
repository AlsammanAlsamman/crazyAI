I take the pile to the Weighing Hall, where the marks are read one at a time into a trough of grey wax kept always at blood heat. Each mark presses its shape into the wax before the last shape has cooled flat — so the trough never holds a clean picture of any single mark, only the sum of every collision so far, the way footprints layer into one churned floor. I keep seven bell-jars of biting insects, each jar tuned to a different hunger, and I dip a wire into the wax after every mark and let each jar's swarm bite the wire in its own count and rhythm; the bite-pattern is carried to a spinning slate wheel that only ever turns forward, never back, so no bite can be walked off once it's landed. When the pile ends, I plunge the wire once into a basin of chilling brine — this is the part that must not be rushed, and I wait, watching the surface, until the frost-lace stops spreading, because a token pulled too early is soft and lies. What the frost leaves is a fixed lattice of six frost-flowers, no two ever alike, and that lattice — sketched onto tin with a stylus, never the wax itself, which I scrape clean and remelt for the next pile — is the token. The wax, the bite-wire, the swarms' hunger, all of it I throw back into the trough afterward; only the tin sketch survives. Change one mark anywhere in the pile and the wax takes the press differently, so the swarms bite differently, so the wheel turns to a different resting notch, so the frost grows a wholly other lattice — the whole flower, not a petal of it, comes out changed.
