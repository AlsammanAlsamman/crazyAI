I lay the pile of marks in their given order upon the counting-bars — the same bars the tensed speak and the woman death lean against, for the bars alone hold true count in this world. Each mark I dip first into the inkwell that never runs dry, then touch it to a fresh child-feather; the feather does not record the mark's shape, only its weight, the way pigment sinks differently for every stroke. That feather I lay across the eight violin strings strung over the landscape, and I pluck the string nearest the mark's own weight — never softly, always the same force, for the force must never vary. The pluck does not stay in one string; the whole instrument answers, every string shivering a little, because the strings are bound under one bridge, and a shudder in one bends the pitch of all the rest before it settles. I do this for every mark, never skipping, never doubling back, and no two marks ever share a feather. Between marks I do not let the strings go fully still — I catch them mid-tremor and carry that tremor forward into the next pluck, so the instrument remembers everything it has already been told, folding it into everything still to come. Only when the last mark has been plucked do I wait — truly wait, hands off — for the storm that channels the sky's dissolving pigment to pass once over the strings; the storm reads the final shivering pattern and dissolves it down into eight small notes, no more, which I write as the token. The feathers, the inkwell's used drops, the mid-tremor counts — all of it I throw to the pickers, who take it apart so nothing of the original order survives in the world above. Change one mark, even the last, and the whole chord the storm reads comes out a different token, unrecognizable from the first, because the tremor never forgets and never forgives a single altered weight.
