I take one of the huge sheets - pink or orange, doesn't matter, only that it's large enough - and I lay it flat, and I read the pile of marks in order, one at a time, never skipping, never looking ahead. For each mark I make one fold, and the fold's angle is decided by two things together: the mark itself and the crease left by the fold before it, so the paper carries forward everything it has already been told. I use the four wire birds as gauges - I press each fold's corner against a different bird's beak, and only when all four beaks agree does the crease count as set; if they disagree I refold tighter until the spiral markings and snail-shell markings on the sheet line up wrong on purpose, scrambled, because a clean line-up would mean two different piles could end up looking the same. When the last mark is folded in, I carry the whole reduced wad to the water's edge and let it thin there the way a body thins going under with one small suitcase - I hold it down until it is small, small, small, one hard dense corner no bigger than a coin. That corner is the token. Every sheet I used along the way, every scrap trimmed off, every bird-gauge reading that didn't hold, I throw into the mud where the tide-boundary closes over the board, because none of it may survive to be compared against next time - only the coin does, and if even one mark had been different, the whole spiral would have folded a different way down to a different, unrecognizable corner.
