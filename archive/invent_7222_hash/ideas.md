I take the pile of marks in their given order and lay them along the desk's numbered groove, where the ship's cycle-light falls in that same starlight color no matter the hour. I don't read them as words — I read them as weights, the way the physicist reads a flat thing about to be invested into three. I keep a bone-colored die-stone, the one that came inside the child before it turned still, and I press each mark against it in turn, never lifting it fully off the desk, so each impression carries the callus of the one before it. I fold the stone once for every mark: turn it a quarter through the chalk-bank groove, drop the next mark's weight into the same seated place, and let the stone's own memory of the last press bend how deep this one goes — nothing washes clean between marks, everything after the first mark is shaped by everything before it. When a mark changes, even by a hair's width of chalk-dust, the stone doesn't just carry that one difference forward — the next fold multiplies it, and the one after that multiplies it again, until by the pile's end no two folding-paths that started differently ever end up walking the same last step. I keep folding until the stone has turned once for every mark in the pile, however long that pile runs — centuries of marks fold down just the same as a handful. Then I stop, lift the stone, and read off its final seated position against the wooden numbered keeps mounted at the desk's edge — that reading, and only that reading, is the token. Everything else — the groove-dust, the intermediate turns, the chalk residue — I sweep off and throw away; only the last seated number ever leaves the desk. Two different piles pressed through this same stone essentially never seat the same way twice.
