I take the pile of marks in their given order and lay them, one by one, onto the coiled limestone trail where the shadow already runs white and arching. I set a sphere at the trail's high mouth — the sphere is my running weight, my only memory — and for each mark I press its shape into the sphere's face before letting it go. It tumbles a fixed count of turns down the mason trail, no more, no fewer, and where it strikes a stalk it sobs once, cracking a hairline into itself, and I read the angle of that crack as the next weight to carry forward. A footprint left at a stalk is a woman's business, not mine, so I never look back at it — only the sphere's new face matters, and the old face I let shrink and go. Where a mark disagrees with what a priest would call true, the whole sphere reshapes, not just a sliver of it — one wrong mark and every stalk downstream sobs differently, the shadow bends elsewhere entire. When the last mark has been pressed in, I don't keep the sphere itself; I take only the last, smallest crack it left in the final stalk — that hairline, small as a token, is what I hand over. Everything else — the tumbled stone dust, the discarded footprints, the eggshells the trail sheds along the way — I sweep off into the jungle's open plumbing and forget on purpose.
