I take the pile of marks and pour them, one by one, into the cup that is never pure — the tavern's sickened wine — because pure wine holds no memory, but wine that is cut remembers every drop added to it. Each mark is a pull on the cup, and after each pull I taste the cup's color against my tongue and let that color become the color waiting for the next pull, so the whole pile passes through as a single unbroken pour, no mark ever judged alone or twice. When the pile ends, I don't stop at the cup — I carry its last color through my own organs, the way a body carries a sickness until every organ compensates, so the color is bent seven times, once per organ, and each bending throws away half of what came before, keeping only what refuses to sit still. I check the result at the garden door, holding it beside the nightingale's last note; if the two don't trade beauty for beauty exactly, I bend again. What comes out is small enough to knot into a jacket's collar, and I test it the only way that matters: I change one mark, anywhere in the pile, even the quietest one, and pour the whole thing through again from the first cup. If the new token still resembles the old one's shape, I throw the whole method away — a knot that remembers its old shape after one thread is pulled is no knot at all, just a door someone forgot to turn.
