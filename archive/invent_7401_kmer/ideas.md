I stretch the strand along a fixed rail of iron pegs, one bead resting in each notch, and beside the rail I keep a frame with exactly k open eyes cut through a plank — a window no wider than k beads that never moves once nailed. I feed the strand through the window one bead at a time, the right side pulling a new bead in, the left side spitting the oldest bead out into a discard trough where it dries and is forgotten. Each time a fresh bead settles into place, the k beads sitting inside the window form one shape, and for that shape I go to my board of hooks — one hook waiting for every possible combination of the four bead-kinds across k slots, carved once, all in rows, standing still ever after, letting light fall on it but never itself moving. On the hook matching the window's current shape I add one knot to the cord already hanging there; the knot is tied blind, by feel, added and never touched again. I do not read a hook until the whole strand has passed through the window and the last bead has dropped into the trough. Only then do I walk the board and count knots on every cord, hook by hook, and that count is the pattern's true total — exact, because no knot was ever untied and no cord double-tied, and because the window showed me every stretch of k beads exactly once, missing none, repeating none. The beads in the trough I burn at day's end, since a bead once slid clear of the window carries no more weight than ash; I keep no memory of the strand itself, only of the shapes it made passing through. What stays is the board of cords; what is thrown away is the strand, bead by bead, the instant its shape has been read.
