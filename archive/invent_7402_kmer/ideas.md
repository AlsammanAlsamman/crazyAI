I lay the strand along the dock rail and set an oar-frame across it — a frame cut to hold exactly k beads and no more, mast-pegs down its length so each bead sits pinned and named by its color: shell, flower-plant, plank-grain, or dent-poison, the four kinds the strand ever carries. Under the frame I read the k beads left to right and cut a small board — a name-plank — whose grain-pattern of notches copies exactly that order of four. I carry the plank to the courtyard, where every plank ever cut stands upright in its own row, sorted by the barrage-stand method: I walk the row, plank in hand, and if its notch-pattern matches a standing plank exactly, I don't set it down — I strike one tally-notch into that standing plank's foot and let my cut one go to the kindling pile, thrown away, intact only long enough to compare. If no plank matches, mine stays standing, foot bare but for the first notch. Then I slide the oar-frame one bead forward — the trailing bead drops off the near end, discarded to the barrel, and a new bead is drawn onto the far peg — and I read the frame again. I do this the whole strand's length, stopping only when the frame runs past the last bead and has no full k to hold. By the end the courtyard stands full of planks, and each plank's foot-notches, counted once, are that pattern's exact total.
