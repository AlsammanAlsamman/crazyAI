I crouch where the strand of beads is drawn taut across the pond's mirror, the wire that the two dark figures walk still trembling from their last crossing, and I let the beads come to me one at a time, threaded past my fingers by the same tension that keeps the walkers upright. I hold a short loop of gut-string across exactly k beads — no more, no less — pinched at both ends between thumb and moss-stained peg, and whatever four colors sit inside that loop I read off as a single word: red, red, bone-white, black, say. For each such word I keep a cairn on the bank, one small stack of colored pebbles per pattern I have already met, arranged in the order the doors along the garden wall open — sideways, never in a row a stranger would call sensible. I walk to the cairn whose order matches my loop's word and lay one more pebble on it; if no cairn yet answers to that word, I begin one, using the reflection in the pond to confirm I have not already started it under a trick of the light. Then I do the necessary throwing-away: the bead at the trailing end of my loop, the one that has finished its turn as the far leg of the tally, I drop through the nearest sideways door into whatever garden it opens onto, and it is gone from my count for good, the same way a plucked note leaves the strand once its ring has died. The loop slides forward exactly one bead, arms flung wide the way the wire-walkers hold themselves against falling, cuff of the loop flapping loose and catching the next color. I do this until the strand ends, and the cairns on the bank, once counted stone by stone, give the exact tally of every pattern that ever passed.
