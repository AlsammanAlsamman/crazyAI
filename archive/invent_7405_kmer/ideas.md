I run the strand along the dry riverbed cut through my clay walls, one bead's width at a time, never letting it pool or double back on itself. The moment the current shows me a stretch of exactly k beads sitting shoulder to shoulder, I press a wet lattice frame down over just those beads — cut once, and I never reshape it, one lattice-width for every reading, no more, no less. Whatever four colors sit inside the frame, in whatever order, I pour clay against them; the clay takes the exact seam of colors behind it, and I lift a crest — a casting — off that stretch, the pattern made solid and mine to carry. I do not look back at the beads once the crest is drawn: they are the first solid thing the shadow trailing the strand meets, and shadow uses them up right there, dissolving them into the wall the instant the frame lifts, so nothing of the strand lingers but its casting. I carry each crest out to the flat desert, the color of locked part, where the horizon permits no makes beyond it — nothing new happens out there, only sorting of what already is. I press the crest's face into the sand to see whether its seam matches a mound already standing. If it matches, I raise no new mound; I set one wildflower seed atop it, and the seed does not grow — nothing grows past that horizon — it only sits, counted by how the sky-dropped roof over the desert dips lower the more seeds gather beneath it. If the crest matches no mound, I plant it fresh and seedless, its own mound, waiting on its first match to come. Rain crosses the desert at intervals and clays the ground behind every mound firmer, so a mound once set can never drift or run together with its neighbor — the sand itself refuses two different seams into one hollow. I throw away only the crest once its seam is checked and its seed placed or its mound founded; that clay returns wet to the wall for the next stretch. What stays, standing when the strand finally runs dry at the riverbed's end, are the mounds alone. Then I walk the row of them, lift each roof, and read off the seed-count settled under every one — the exact tally of every pattern the strand ever carried.
