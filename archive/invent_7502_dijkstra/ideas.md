I take the traveler's own wooden stand — the rented one, the half-finished kind that any stranger can detach and reset — and I drive it into the ground at the traveler's place among the others. Every other place gets its own peg, set out across the suncity's suitcase-shaped square, and between pegs that share a road I run a hollow reed, cut so its bore-length matches the road's true walking-length, never guessed. Then I do what the season taught the districts to do: I pour the same water into every reed mouth at the traveler's peg at once, and I let it drive itself — piped, not carried, because piped is what moves everything here. Where two or more streams would meet a hollow at the same place, I only let the first wet mark stand; I scrape the later ones off with a fingernail before they dry, the way you'd clean a stand for the next crowd. Where a reed runs to a place and only wets its own inside without ever reaching a far peg — a road that goes nowhere useful — I pull that reed out whole and lay it aside, dry, unclaimed, like the tavern's picture nobody finishes. I wait only as long as the slowest wetted reed takes to darken, watching the pegboard the way you'd watch a child warm a bed that isn't theirs. When the water stops climbing anywhere new, I walk the board and read off, peg by peg, how far the dark reached and which reed brought it there — that reed is the way. Pegs the water never touched, I mark hollow, and tell the traveler plainly: no road home from here.
