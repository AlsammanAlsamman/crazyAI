I lay a slab of river clay for every place the traveler might reach, and press a shallow bowl into each one where a stone can rest. From the traveler's own place I stretch cords to every place a road truly runs, knotting a bead into each cord for every span of ground it crosses — three beads, ten beads, however long — and any road that goes nowhere useful I let hang slack, cordless, unclaimed. I set a white stone in the traveler's own bowl, for he is already there, having cost nothing. Then I watch the shadow-stick in the yard: each time its shadow crosses a scratch cut into the brick, I look over every clay slab still holding only a grey stone, not yet settled, and see which cord, counted bead by bead out from a white-stoned slab, would reach it shortest. That cord I follow, I lay a white stone in the new bowl, and I chalk on its rim the count of beads it cost to arrive — the number itself, not the road, is what the traveler keeps. Any cord that would have named a longer count than the chalk already sitting there, I cut loose and feed to the fire; a thing already proven true need not be argued a second time. I go on this way, slab by slab, white stone by white stone, until every bowl holds one — or until a slab is left with no cord ever having reached it, in which case its bowl stays empty and its rim unchalked, and that place the traveler simply cannot get to, and I do not pretend otherwise. When the shadow has crossed the last scratch on the brick and every reachable bowl has gone white, I read the chalked rims aloud to the traveler, slab by slab, and that is his answer.
