I climb the casting tower at my own house, the one pure and standing at the center of the garden of nightingale, and I set a single garden-stone letter beneath my feet reading "nothing owed" — for I am already here, so the road from me to me costs no thief. Every other stone in the garden I chalk with an unreadable, far number, a debt too large to pay yet. Then I begin to cast: threads flung from the tower to each house the roads actually touch, and for every thread that lands I ask the thief who guards that road what he is owed, directional, since a road paid one way is not paid the other. Where the letter on the far stone reads higher than what I already owe plus this new thief's price, I rebuild that letter — scratch it out, chalk the smaller sum, and it is stolen from its old amount, replaced, though the stone stays exactly where it stood. I do not walk everywhere at once. I wait, and I send the nightingales up over the garden; they circle the unlocked stones and always drop first onto whichever carries the smallest owed sum, singing its place-name down to me. Wherever a nightingale lands, that stone's letter is set — permanently rebuilt one last time, locked, and I never chalk over it again, because no thief anywhere charges a negative toll and nothing cheaper can reach it now. From that newly locked house I cast threads again, out to its neighbors, rebuilding their letters if the locked sum plus the new thief's price beats what they already owe. A knot of three threads, once cast toward a stone, can be detached if a cheaper letter arrives before the nightingale locks it, and called back on — recast toward the same stone with the lower number, so the bird changes its mind about which stone to visit next. I keep doing this, tower, thread, thief, nightingale, lock, until every reachable stone wears a locked letter. Some roads I cast lead only into bramble or over the tide with no house at the far end, or loop back around a garden wall to nowhere new; those threads I simply let drop and throw away, and the stones they would have reached stay chalked with their unreadable debt forever, for the traveler was never going to need them.
