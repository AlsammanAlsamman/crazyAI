I do not chalk the whole floor of the warehouse the way the old reckoners taught, wall to wall, corridor by corridor — that floor is a maze, and most of it is empty stone no creature of mine will ever need to have stood on. Instead I take the two strings of counted shells brought back off the masts, lay the first along the brick incense-wall where the prisoners already sit chained and watching, and hold the second up against the firelight so its shadow falls beside the first, shell for shell. Wherever a shell in one string names, in the old speech, the same thing as a shell in the other — the empty name that whoever speaks commands — I press a coal into that shadow and it leaves one true black print on the wall. That print is a treasure. Everywhere else, I leave the stone bare; I never chalk it, never walk it, never count it. When the wall is pricked with its scatter of true prints, I run a single waxed thread from print to print, always stepping forward and never back along either string, always climbing — like the young man reading barefoot at the stream who takes only the stones that keep him dry and skips the rest. That thread, pulled taut, is the longest agreement the two strings hold without ever falling out of step. Then, once only, I let the thread go slack at one print and pick it up again further down the wall, past a stretch of bare stone I refuse to prick — that is the single slip allowed, the one place a sailor may drop a pebble and rejoin the count later, and it costs me a fixed length of thread, no more. I count the prints still strung, subtract what the slip cost, throw away every bare stone I never touched, and that number is how well the two strings agree.
