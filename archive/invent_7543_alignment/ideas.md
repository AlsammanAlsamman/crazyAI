I lay the two files of marks each down its own chessboard, square by square, rank by rank, pawns already standing on every mark. I walk both boards at once, rank against rank, and at each rank I hold up one hand from board one and one hand from board two and see whether they close together as a true pair — sticks matched, nothing unpaired. Where the hands close, the pawn on that square crosses cleanly and is promoted to a queen, and I feed that queen's worth to my chest, to the waiting port, as a breath of oxygen taken in clean. Where the hands don't close — an odd, orphaned hand left holding its stick alone — that's trouble, and I feed it to the same port as ash to be carried off, not kept. I run this reckoning twice over, side by side, like two hearts beating in the same chest: one heart walks the boards rank for rank with no give in the count, the other lets the second board's boy climb once into the oak at exactly one rank and not come back down — meaning from that rank forward every mark on board two answers to the mark one square along on board one, a single permanent slip and never a second one, because a boy only climbs once. Both hearts keep counting queens crowned and ash carried to the end of the boards. Then I set the two hearts beside each other and keep whichever delivered more oxygen and less ash — the shifted heart if the boy's climb bought back more matched hands than it cost in the rank it abandoned, the unshifted heart if it didn't. Every other kind of slip — climbing twice, climbing backward, climbing partway and returning — I throw off the board entirely; only the one true, one-way climb is ever weighed. The number I hand back is that winning heart's tally, queens minus ash, and once reckoned it is sealed the way a promised friendship is sealed over wine each year among the stars — spoken once, fixed, not reopened, good until the next two files are ever laid down together again.
