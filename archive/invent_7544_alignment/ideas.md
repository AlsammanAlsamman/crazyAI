I take the two strings as they truly are here — cords of knotted bridge-rope, each knot a shell-bead of one of the four or five kinds the guild trades in, and I stretch them side by side across the gap between two drifting districts, over open water where a chained sea-beast idles and waits. I walk the bridge bead by bead, thumb on one cord, forefinger on the other, and where a bead of one cord sits square opposite its match on the other I press them together and chalk a mark on the slat an errand-boy carries behind me. Where they don't agree, I have three things I can do and must choose the cheapest: cut a bead free and let it drop to the beast below, pay out a loop of slack into one cord so it walks ahead or behind its partner for a stretch — this is the slipping, the letting one string fall out of step — or simply swap a chalk mark for a drop of ink, which dries dark and costs more than chalk. I never choose twice for the same step without weighing what the errand-boy's slat already shows for the steps behind it, because the cheapest path to any point is the only one worth keeping; the rest I throw to the water. At the far rail I stop, count chalk against ink and dropped beads, and that count, read aloud once and entered in the cellar ledger, is the number that says how well the two strings agree.
