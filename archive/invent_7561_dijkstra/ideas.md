I bring the traveler to the standing-stone hub where every road begins, and I write his name on the calendar's first day, for reason knocks at the sound of a name being fixed, and once fixed it never moves. From that stone I send a chant down each road at once — not walking it myself, for the border keeps moving and the eye cannot follow a chant's flight — and each chant breaks into a bird the instant it reaches the road's far end, its throat holding the exact length in cord-handspans it crossed. I do not watch the birds; I wait for the freeze. When the whole field freezes at the sound of a bird landing, that is my sign one has arrived, and of every bird now perched and unclaimed I take the one whose chant is shortest and nail its name to the next day on the calendar — that place is settled, closed, never opened again. From that newly nailed day I send fresh chants onward down its roads, each carrying its bird's length added to whatever the road adds, and any chant that would land on a day already nailed I let die in the air unheard, for reading twice over settled script only glows and steals the reader's name. Some roads, I know before I start, run into the maze that goes forever unvisited — lead to gates that face inward, to doors with the family's tray still full outside them, morning after morning, no chant ever answering back — and for these no bird ever breaks free to land, so their days on the calendar stay blank forever, and I throw the thread away rather than let it hang wanting. I keep at this, freeze after freeze, nailing one more day each time, until every reachable place carries its name and its length, the calendar's carved rim full round to where it can go and no further, and the traveler reads the rim once, plainly, to know every road home.
