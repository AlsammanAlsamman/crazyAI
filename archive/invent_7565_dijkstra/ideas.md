I take the traveler to the boy knotted into the oak, since he already holds the whole cloth of roads looped over his branches, and I have him spread it flat across the grass — one waxed thread for every road, thin and long where a road winds, thick and short where it runs easy, exactly as long as the road is hard. At the traveler's own knot I bind a scholar-bird's down into a wick and touch one spark to it the instant the sun's rim clears the hill. The fire does not leap from knot to knot; it crawls, down every thread at once, at the same unhurried pace along each one, because that is the only law thread-fire keeps in my world. Wherever the crawling flame first reaches a knot, I press a bead of hardened duck-fat into the bark there — that bead is the road's true length, and nothing more is owed to that knot. Should a second flame come wandering in later along some longer thread, I pinch it dead between wet fingers and feed the blackened thread to the fire already burning in the hunter's clearing; it has nothing left to teach the cloth. Beads fill in nearest-first, the way the folded balloon-cloth always creases close before far, until the flame simply runs out of thread to crawl. Knots left dark and beadless — roads that only loop back on themselves — I leave dark on purpose, and tell the traveler plainly not to trust them. Then I read the beads off, oak to boy to bead to man.

SEED: Equal-paced fire crawling simultaneously down every waxed thread encodes uniform-speed traversal proportional to road length.
SEED: A duck-fat bead fixed at a knot's first flame-arrival records the shortest distance and forever refuses later, longer arrivals.
SEED: Unlit, beadless knots at the end mark roads that lead nowhere reachable, discarded rather than reported.
