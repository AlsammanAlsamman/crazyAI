I take the two strings of symbols and set them along two edges of the round table, and the girls with ground faces mark each square of the grid with the letter it must answer to, using chalk cut from the dirt first that bars from one heart to the other. Then I do not touch square against square one at a time — I lay down a single cord, one cell wide, and let it crawl. It sets its first ring at the corner where both strings begin, and from there it may point any of the eight ways the table allows: forward along one thread, sideways along the other, aslant through both at once, or back upon a ring it has already worn into the stone, curling there to mark a place where one string was let to slip a step while the other held its ground. At every square the cord touches, it does not merely pass — it presses a small weight into the ground there, a coin of agreement or disagreement between the two letters meeting at that ring, and this weight it carries forward, adding or spending it as it goes, so that the cord's whole body, coiled behind its own head across the table, holds the tally of everywhere it has been, not just where it stands now. The windows and the cylinders around the table stay exactly still throughout — they are the fixed households of past squares, already visited, and I never ask the cord to return to one for its own sake, only to loop through it again when a slip must be recorded, a brief backward curl before it presses on toward the far corner. I wait until the cord has reached the last ring, the corner where both strings run out together, its whole coiled length now lying somewhere across the grid in one unbroken skin, never lifted, never restarted. When a slip light fires — the send that flares whenever the cord curls back upon itself — I note it and let the cord keep crawling; I do not stop to compare anything separately, because the cord is already comparing by moving. Only at the very end do I read the weight carried in the cord's final ring, the one number it has gathered across its entire journey through every square it wore a mark into. Then I sweep the chalk from the stone, unwind and discard every ring the cord left behind, and keep only that last coin — the number that says how well the two strings agreed, slip and all.
