I take the shape out to the grass field under the window where the city repeats itself exactly, day for day, and I count the blades along the shape's edge — one blade for every position that can be chosen or left out, nothing more, nothing less. Because the city never drifts, I know each blade answers only two ways: pressed flat or left standing, on or off, and there is no bending it partway the way wind bends grass into ripples — that soft rippling belongs to gentler places, not mine. So each dawn, before the day resets, I walk the row and press blades down according to one single arrangement, then I breathe on the window glass until it fogs and hold the fog there, reading it back as a strip of flat stripes — pressed, standing, standing, pressed — and that reading is one pattern caught and set aside. Then my breath runs out, the city freezes into its gasp, and everything snaps back: blades rise on their own, fog clears, the window is blind again and ready. I check the mirror before starting the next day, because the mirror raider goes furious the instant a fogged reading repeats itself, and a furious mirror means I throw that day's pressing away and choose differently. I keep walking the row, one day, one pressing, one fogged reading at a time, refusing nothing that hasn't yet been tried, until every possible way of choosing which blades stand and which lie flat has been pressed, breathed, and read — and only then, with no unrepeated days left to spend, do I stop.
