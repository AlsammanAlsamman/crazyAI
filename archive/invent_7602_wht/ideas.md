I walk the ribs inside the gold, counting them from the elephant's trunk to the last curl of lotus petal, because ribs never move and give me a fixed order for positions — one rib, one place in the pattern. Then I go out to the garden, where the straight path runs between rows of leaves, one leaf standing for each rib. Under the full moon every leaf is painted separately, whole and sharp-edged, no leaf half-lit — this is the only kind of light the moon gives here, entire or absent, and that is the stripe I need: on or off, nothing between. To try every way of choosing which ribs to include, I walk the straight path over and over, and each time I pass I turn exactly one leaf from shadow to moonlight or moonlight to shadow — never more than one — so that by the time I have walked it enough times, every leaf has stood in every combination with every other leaf, once each, no combination missed and none repeated; the black at the end of the straight is how I know a full round is finished, because a black is the only way out and I only reach it once I've turned every leaf back to shadow again. Whatever pattern the leaves hold when I stop mid-walk, I carry into the gold and cut it straight into a rib as a stripe, fully raised where the leaf was lit, flat where it was dark, and it stays there, steady, needing no more moonlight to hold its shape. Then I go back out, because in the garden no jewel exists apart from the light it borrows from the others, and I let the jewels along the path pass their light hand to hand until one comes up dark — that dark jewel tells me nothing more remains to try, and I stop. The dark figure with the flute plays one note each time a rib is finished; when the elephant lifts its ear at silence instead, I throw the leaves' ash into the black and walk away.
