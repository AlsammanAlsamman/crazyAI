I take the message as a file of small river-stones, each notched with its own mark, and lay them one to a house along the cross-marked board where the carved flickering line keeps its watch at the center. I do not write the seal by copying — I combine. Two stones are held together over the tower's guard-line and set down as one: if their notches match, the new stone comes back bare, erased, for that is the arithmetic here, an adding that undoes itself. I walk the wider circle of snake pieces, tightening from every edge at once the way the name-day tightens toward the year's closing, and at each turn of the spiral I gather a different clutch of stones — never the same clutch twice, so that each seal-stone I carve at the tail answers for its own crossing of houses, the way the calendar's mason hallway lets one casting fall into many rivers without confusing them. I carve as many trailing stones as the board has snake-loops, no more, and set them past the last message-stone the way the snake sets its tail past its own head, closing the circle on itself. What I throw away are the guide-scratches made mid-combining — temporary notches held only to keep a clutch's place while my hands were full — because the board remembers nothing of process, only the final carved stone. I wait, then, for the twigs to prove themselves: every twig alive today is exactly as old as every other, and only when the seal-stones sit as still and untouched as those twigs do I trust the seal is true. Should a river later take a stone, or a careless foot scatter one from its house, I do not mourn it — I lay the surviving message-stones and the trailing seal-stones back into their houses and combine again along the same spiral; where a house stands empty, the erasing arithmetic hands the missing notch back whole, since what made the seal, added again, only returns what is missing.
