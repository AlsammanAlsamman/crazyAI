Every lantern in my row is glass-walled twice over: an amber pane facing the road that holds the bright wick, and a smoked pane facing the cellar-side that holds the black wick, and each wick burns to a height that is a number — the amber taller or shorter gives the bright strength, the smoked wick's crawl up or down its own scale (a strength can run negative, burning inward, eating its own wax) gives the shadow strength. I never touch the row myself until the tide-bell rings and a district's rope bridge locks level against its neighbors — that's the one breath in the whole slow drift when the flames hold still enough to be read true, and I've learned to do nothing before it and everything within it. Then I walk the row with the Arbiter's rod, a black rosewood switch capped in mirror-silver at both ends, and the rod itself decides which two lanterns it wants that night: it warms faintly in my hand over a true pairing, stays cold over all the rest, and forcing it early only earns a burned palm and a wrong ledger. When it warms, I hold the mirror-caps facing each other across the chosen pair, and light and shadow leap the gap like a blade catching moonlight. If both wicks lean the same way — bright feeding bright, or shadow feeding shadow — the receiving flame climbs taller and greedier while the giving one gutters low without dying; that's the debt growing, the ledger owing more. But if a bright wick on one lantern matches a shadow wick of exactly the same height on the other, the mirror-caps flash white once and both wicks go out together in the same breath — equal fire, equal dark, spent on each other. I pinch the two dead wicks free and carry them down to the cellar nobody visits, where every cancelled pair rots slowly into the ink that outlasts empires, and I never relight a snuffed lantern; paid is paid, and it stays dark until someone strikes fresh wax into it on its own account. Whatever the rod refuses to warm to that night I leave standing exactly as it burns, its numbers untouched, waiting its turn on some later tide — I've known men too impatient for that waiting who reached for a pairing the rod hadn't chosen, fingers brushing a sleeve, closing on empty air, brushing cloth again, and got nothing for it but a scorched hand and a debt no worse and no better than before.
