I walk the row at dusk, when the lanterns burn lowest and their two flames show clearest: the white-gold wick that leans outward, bright-strength, and the grey wick curled inward, shadow-strength, each free to lean left, meaning positive, or right, meaning negative, on any given night. I carry my antler crook, cut from the ram that wandered farthest from the flock, because only antler holds the pairing-thread without fraying it — that thread is the same line that runs stranger to stranger through the town, the one nobody sees until a hand tugs it. I hook the crook through the thread between two lanterns the town has already marked for visiting — never lanterns I choose myself, only ones the drips already point toward — and pull them close until their wicks touch. Touching, I hold my breath and watch the wax: I keep a flat grinding-stone at my feet, scraped clean of moss, and let the two flames drip onto it together. If both drips run the same color, both gold or both grey, they pool into one fatter bead, and I press that bead back into whichever lantern leans stronger, so the pair adds and one lantern now carries the weight of both. If the drips run opposite colors, gold meeting grey in exact measure, they hiss white on the stone, curl to ash, and both wicks go dark at once — I never relight them; a canceled wick relit is bad luck older than the grass itself. I feed that ash to the sheep circling the row, since sheep will eat what fire has already argued over and nothing else will touch it. I keep fresh grass-stalks pulled from the field beside me, one for every lantern, and lay a stalk flat wherever a pair has been visited, so I never walk the same two lanterns twice in one night — a bent stalk means skip it and move on. What stays still through all of this is the row itself; I never move the lanterns, only the thread, the crook, and my own feet between them. I wait for the stone to cool fully between each pairing, because a hot stone lies about the true color of a drip, and a lie there could tell me to add when I should have canceled, or the reverse. By the time every grass-stalk lies flat, the row holds only lanterns that have added into fewer, fatter flames, and a scatter of dark, wickless husks I gather at dawn and bury beneath the white dress left hanging on the fence, since the dead wicks belong to whoever wore that dress last.
