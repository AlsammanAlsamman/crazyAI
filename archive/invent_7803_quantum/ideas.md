I lay the lanterns down the long trench behind the foundry, wick to wick, and I read the visiting rule off the foreman's chalk-slate before I touch anything, because the pairs it calls are never neighbours — they're matched by the pitch of the hum each lantern gives off, and I have to walk the whole row with my ear to the glass before I know who's paired with whom tonight. For each named pair I lift both lanterns onto the twin scales bolted to the workbench, bright-wick basin on the left arm, shadow-wick basin on the right, and I pour their strengths as measured coal-dust into the pans — bright dust silver-grey, shadow dust the black soot skimmed off a snuffed flame. Where a pair's dust matches in colour, I sweep both pans into the same crucible and it flares once, brighter, fused. Where the colours oppose — silver against black — I brush them together with a badger-hair whisk over the drain-grate, and they hiss out to nothing, and I let that hiss finish all the way before moving on, because a lantern rushed while still hissing relights wrong. What stays still is the row itself — I never carry a lantern from its position, only its measured dust — and what I throw away is the swept ash off the drain-grate, which I never weigh again, it's spent. I wait, every third pair, for the trench-draft to steady, since a stray gust can tip a scale-pan mid-pour and give me a false balance, and I'd rather stand there a minute than relight a whole segment. When the slate's last pair is done, I walk the row once more end to end with the tallow-lamp, checking no basin still trembles, and only then do I chalk the new totals beside each lantern's foot.
