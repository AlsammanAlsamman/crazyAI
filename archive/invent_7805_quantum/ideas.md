I walk the row with a bellows slung at my hip and a bone flute hung at my collarbone, and I read each lantern by pressing two fingers to its glass: one finger finds the tall bright flame, the other finds the flat shadow-flame that burns backward and gives no light, only weight. The pairing rule reaches me as breath, never as sight — I lift the flute and blow one long note down the row, and wherever the note forks and bends double, those two lanterns belong together for this turn. Between paired lanterns I lay a hollow reed-cone, the kind carried like the ones on cliff-milestones marking the road's forks. If the bright flame and the shadow flame that meet through the cone are the same throat-size, I tip the cone and let them touch — both flames die together, wick and shadow-wick, and I sweep the cooled glass into the cellar-sea where spent things go, never to be fished back. If they are not equal, I do not force the cancel — I hold the bellows still and breathe once more through the flute, pouring the smaller into the larger until only the leftover glow sits in one glass, and that leftover I carve fresh onto the beige milestone-spine before evening reaches the surface, because after dark the row forgets which lanterns were ever paired at all. What burns on, burns alone until the next breath finds it.
