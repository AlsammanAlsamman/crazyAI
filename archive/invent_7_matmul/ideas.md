I take the first great table and stand it as a wall of corridors, each corridor a row, and every door down its length carved with one of its numbers. I take the second table and lay it crosswise as a second wall, its corridors now columns, each door there carved with its own number, so that where a corridor of the first wall would pass through a corridor of the second, there is a meeting-place, and at every meeting-place I set a vase, empty, waiting. Then I open bellows — one bellow for every shared index the two tables hold — and pump mud, not air, through the doors: mud is patient, it takes the shape of the door it passes and carries the number carved there stamped into it like a seal. Where two seals arrive at the same vase, from the row-door and the column-door that share an index, the mud does not just pool — it multiplies its two seals into one heavier clot and drops that clot in. I wait, corridor by corridor, until every bellow feeding a given meeting-place has fired once — a barrage of three, or a thousand, however many indices the tables share — and only then do I read the vase, because a vase read early lies. Once full, I measure its fill line; that is my new number, and I write it into the third table at the crossing the vase marked. Then I tip the vase, throw the settled mud back to the corridor floor, wipe it clean, and it stands ready for the next crossing's cargo. The doors and corridors never move; only the mud moves, only the bellows breathe, and a well-aimed cargo of a thousand parts arrives at each vase in its own order but is only ever trusted once it is whole.
