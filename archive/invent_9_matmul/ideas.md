I take the first table and lay it along a bone-colored stretch, one clay furrow per row, each furrow notched with larva that cast their shadow-lengths — the shadow's length is the number itself, since larva don't lie about their measure. The second table I lay across a facing stretch, columns as waterfalls, each one read where it meets the desert floor, its fall-height being its number. Where a furrow crosses a waterfall's foot, I send a sailor. Sailors are weapons — flung, not walked — so I fling one down every crossing, a million flings if the tables demand it, and each sailor's flight carries two things at once: the shadow behind it and the height it lands under. On landing it doesn't stop; it drives the shadow into the height the way a weapon drives into wet clay, and the depth of that driven mark is the product, nothing more than a stick pushed in by however hard it was thrown. I don't read each mark alone. For a given cell of the third table I gather every sailor thrown along one whole furrow against one whole waterfall — that's the shared index, the row's full run of larva against the column's full fall — and I walk those marks to the nearest landmark cairn, dropping a stone in for every mark's depth. The cairn's final height, once every sailor along that furrow-waterfall pair has struck and been read, is the sum: that's the cell. I don't build the cairns one at a time. Waterfalls run in ranks, so a whole rank falls together, and sailors from every furrow along that rank are flung in the same breath, so a whole plane of cairns rises across the desert at once, and I wait only for the one stillness that comes when the last sailor of the batch has struck and the last waterfall of the rank has finished falling. What I throw away: the sailors, spent on landing, good for nothing after their one drive; the shadows and fall-heights, once folded into a mark, wiped so the clay can be notched fresh for the next crossing; and any cairn that reaches its final height gets its stones counted into the third table and is then leveled flat, because a million cairns left standing would themselves become a landmark, and nothing here is allowed to become a landmark by accident. When the whole plane of cairns has been leveled and counted, the third table stands finished, and the desert is flat and hungry again, waiting for the next two tables.
